📌 Chapter snapshot

  • Acids taste sour, turn blue litmus red and give H⁺ (H₃O⁺) ions in water. Bases taste bitter, feel soapy, turn red litmus blue and give OH⁻ ions. Bases that dissolve in water are alkalis.
  • Indicators: litmus, turmeric (natural); phenolphthalein, methyl orange (synthetic); onion, vanilla, clove (olfactory).
  • Acid + metal → salt + H₂. Acid + metal carbonate/hydrogencarbonate → salt + CO₂ + H₂O. Acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation). Metal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic.
  • pH scale 0–14: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 basic. Lower pH = more H⁺ = stronger acid.
  • pH matters: digestion (HCl in stomach, antacids), tooth decay below pH 5.5, soil, stings, acid rain (pH < 5.6).
  • Salts from strong acid + strong base are neutral; strong acid + weak base acidic; weak acid + strong base basic.
  • From common salt: NaOH (chlor-alkali), bleaching powder CaOCl₂, baking soda NaHCO₃, washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O. Plaster of Paris CaSO₄·½H₂O from gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O.
  • CBSE weightage: unit Chemical Substances (Ch 1–4) carries 25 marks. This chapter usually gets 2 to 3 MCQs plus a 2-mark and a 3-mark question (often on pH or a salt's preparation and uses).
2.1

Acids, bases and indicators

Acids are sour in taste and change the colour of blue litmus to red. Bases are bitter, soapy to touch, and change red litmus to blue. Litmus is a natural indicator (a purple dye extracted from lichen). Substances that show by their colour or smell whether a solution is acidic or basic are called indicators.

Type of indicatorExamplesHow it shows
Naturallitmus, turmeric, red cabbage, hydrangea, petunia and geranium petalscolour change
Syntheticphenolphthalein, methyl orangecolour change
Olfactoryonion, vanilla essence, clove oilsmell disappears in a base (stays in an acid)

🤔 The turmeric stain mystery

A curry stain on a white cloth is yellow. Wash it with soap (a base) and it turns reddish-brown. Rinse it with plenty of water and it becomes yellow again. Turmeric is a natural indicator!

🧪 Try itMake red cabbage indicator

  1. Chop a few red cabbage leaves, boil them in water for 10 minutes and strain the purple liquid.
  2. Pour a little into five cups. Add lemon juice, vinegar, baking soda solution, soap solution and plain water.
  3. Record the colours: acids turn it pink-red, bases green-yellow. Arrange the cups from most acidic to most basic.
2.1.2

How acids and bases react

2.1.2 Acids and bases with metals

Activity 2.3: zinc granules + dilute sulphuric acid. Hydrogen gas forms. When the gas is passed through soap solution, the bubbles burn with a pop sound near a flame.

Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas · Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑

Some metals also react with bases to give hydrogen. Zinc with hot sodium hydroxide gives sodium zincate: 2NaOH(aq) + Zn(s) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂(g) (CBSE 2024 MCQ). Not all metals do this.

2.1.3 Metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates with acids

Metal carbonate / hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + CO₂ + H₂O
  • Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
  • NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
  • Limestone, chalk and marble are all forms of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), so they too give CO₂ with acids.

2.1.4 Acids and bases react with each other (neutralisation)

Activity 2.6: take NaOH solution with 2 drops of phenolphthalein (pink). Add dilute HCl drop by drop: the pink colour disappears. Add a drop of NaOH: pink comes back. The effect of a base is cancelled by an acid and vice versa.

Base + Acid → Salt + Water · NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

2.1.5 Metallic oxides with acids

Activity 2.7: add dilute HCl to black copper oxide and stir. The solution turns blue-green because copper(II) chloride forms: CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O. Metal oxides react with acids like bases do, so they are called basic oxides.

2.1.6 Non-metallic oxides with bases

Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. This is like base + acid → salt + water, so non-metal oxides such as CO₂ are acidic oxides.

⚠️ Watch out

  • Hydrogen is not given off when most metals react with nitric acid: HNO₃ is a strong oxidising agent and oxidises the H₂ to water (CBSE 2024 A–R, Ch 3).
  • Pass CO₂ for too long and lime water turns clear again: CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ → Ca(HCO₃)₂ (soluble).

🧪 Try itEggshell fizz

  1. Drop a piece of eggshell (calcium carbonate) into a glass of vinegar.
  2. Bubbles of CO₂ form on the shell within minutes. After a day the shell becomes soft.
  3. Write the word equation: calcium carbonate + acetic acid → calcium acetate + carbon dioxide + water.

2.2

What do all acids and all bases have in common?

All acids give the same reactions because they all produce hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq), in solution. All bases produce hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq).

Why is water needed? (Activity 2.9)

Dry HCl gas does not change the colour of dry blue litmus paper, but it turns moist blue litmus red. Acids show acidic behaviour only in the presence of water, because only then do they give H⁺ ions:

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ · H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion)

H⁺ cannot exist alone; it attaches to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion. Similarly, bases in water: NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻; KOH → K⁺ + OH⁻; Mg(OH)₂ → Mg²⁺ + 2OH⁻.

🔑 Alkali vs base

Bases that are soluble in water are called alkalis (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis: Cu(OH)₂ and Fe(OH)₃ are bases that do not dissolve. Alkalis are soapy, bitter and corrosive: never taste or touch them.

Neutralisation in terms of ions: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).

Dilution

Dilution decreases the concentration of H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ ions per unit volume.

🧪 Try itDry vs wet litmus

  1. Take two strips of blue litmus paper (from a school lab or chemist). Keep one dry and moisten the other with a drop of water.
  2. Hold both just above the mouth of an open bottle of vinegar for a minute (do not touch the liquid).
  3. Only the wet strip turns red. Explain why, using the idea that acids give H₃O⁺ ions only in water.
2.3

How strong are acid or base solutions? The pH scale

A universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators. It shows different colours at different concentrations of hydrogen ions. The scale used to measure the H⁺ concentration is the pH scale (p stands for potenz, German for power). It runs from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very alkaline).

pHNatureIonsExamples
0 to below 7acidic[H⁺] > [OH⁻]gastric juice ≈ 1.2, lemon ≈ 2.2, vinegar ≈ 3, tomato juice ≈ 4.2, milk ≈ 6.5 (curd is lower than milk)
7neutral[H⁺] = [OH⁻]pure water, NaCl solution
above 7 to 14basic (alkaline)[OH⁻] > [H⁺]blood ≈ 7.4, baking soda ≈ 8.5, milk of magnesia ≈ 10, lime water ≈ 12, NaOH ≈ 14

🔑 Strong vs weak

Acids that give many H⁺ ions (ionise completely) are strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃. Acids that give fewer H⁺ ions (partly ionise) are weak acids: CH₃COOH, citric acid, carbonic acid. Likewise NaOH and KOH are strong bases, NH₄OH is a weak base. (CBSE 2024 asked exactly this difference.)

Strong/weak is about ionisation. Concentrated/dilute is about how much acid is in a given volume of water. These are different ideas.

2.3.1 Importance of pH in everyday life

  • Plants and animals: our body works within a narrow pH range of about 7.0 to 7.8. Rain with pH below 5.6 is acid rain; when it flows into rivers it lowers their pH and makes life difficult for aquatic animals.
  • Venus: its atmosphere has thick white and yellowish clouds of sulphuric acid. Life cannot survive there.
  • Soil: plants need a specific pH. Farmers add lime to acidic soil.
  • Digestive system: the stomach makes HCl that helps digestion without harming the stomach. Extra acid causes pain and irritation; an antacid (a mild base like milk of magnesia, Mg(OH)₂) neutralises it.
  • Tooth decay: starts when the pH in the mouth falls below 5.5. Tooth enamel (calcium hydroxyapatite) is the hardest substance in the body but it corrodes in acid. Bacteria make acids from sugar left in the mouth. Basic toothpastes neutralise the acid.
  • Self-defence by animals and plants: bee stings inject an acid (methanoic acid) that causes pain; rub a mild base like baking soda. Nettle leaves have stinging hairs with methanoic acid; the traditional remedy is rubbing a dock plant leaf, which often grows nearby.

2.4

More about salts

A salt is formed when an acid reacts with a base. The positive part comes from the base and the negative part from the acid: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O.

2.4.1 Family of salts

Salts having the same positive or negative radical belong to the same family. NaCl and Na₂SO₄ are in the sodium family; NaCl and KCl are in the chloride family.

2.4.2 pH of salts

Worked example

Na₂CO₃ comes from H₂CO₃ (weak acid) and NaOH (strong base), so its solution is basic (pH > 7). NH₄Cl comes from HCl (strong) and NH₄OH (weak), so it is acidic (pH < 7).

2.4.3

Chemicals from common salt; are crystals of salts really dry?

Common salt (NaCl) is made from sea water, and rock salt is mined from beds formed when ancient seas dried up. Common salt is the raw material for many everyday chemicals.

Sodium hydroxide: the chlor-alkali process

ChemicalFormulaHow it is madeUses
Bleaching powderCaOCl₂chlorine on dry slaked lime: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂Obleaching cotton, linen and wood pulp; bleaching washed clothes; oxidising agent in industry; disinfecting drinking water
Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate)NaHCO₃NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃baking powder (with tartaric acid), antacid, soda-acid fire extinguishers, faster cooking

Plaster of ParisCaSO₄·½H₂Oheat gypsum at 373 K: CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂Osupporting fractured bones, toys, decoration, smooth surfaces

🍰 Baking soda in the kitchen

On heating: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. The CO₂ makes bread and cake soft and spongy. Baking powder = baking soda + a mild edible acid such as tartaric acid. When heated or mixed with water: NaHCO₃ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O + sodium salt of the acid. The tartaric acid neutralises the bitter Na₂CO₃ that baking soda alone would leave.

⚠️ Watch out

  • Plaster of Paris must be stored in a moisture-proof container: with water it sets into a hard mass of gypsum (CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O).
  • Don't heat gypsum above 373 K: it loses all water to become dead-burnt plaster (anhydrous CaSO₄), which does not set.
  • Bleaching powder is often written as CaOCl₂, but the actual composition is complex. Use CaOCl₂ in the exam.

⚠️ Top 8 mistakes students make

  1. Saying a lower pH means a weaker acid. Lower pH = more H⁺ = stronger acid.
  2. Writing "add water to acid". Always add acid to water.
  3. Calling every base an alkali. Only water-soluble bases are alkalis.
  4. Mixing up baking soda (NaHCO₃) and washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).
  5. Writing PoP as CaSO₄·2H₂O. That is gypsum; PoP is CaSO₄·½H₂O.
  6. Saying "strong acid" when you mean "concentrated acid". Strength is about ionisation.
  7. Saying phenolphthalein turns pink in acid. It is colourless in acid, pink in base.
  8. Forgetting the gas tests: H₂ pops; CO₂ turns lime water milky.

CBSE exam pattern and how marks are given

The CBSE Class 10 Science (086) paper is 80 marks, 3 hours, 39 questions: Section A has 20 one-mark questions (16 MCQs and 4 assertion–reason), Section B 6 two-mark, Section C 7 three-mark, Section D 3 five-mark, and Section E 3 four-mark case studies. Unit Chemical Substances: Nature and Behaviour (Chapters 1–4) carries 25 marks.

✍️ How marks are given

  • Preparation of a salt: 1 mark for the balanced equation, ½ to 1 for the condition (e.g. 373 K for PoP), ½ to 1 for each use.
  • pH questions: the value or order earns ½; the reason (H⁺ concentration) earns the rest.
  • Observation questions: name the gas and its test for full marks.

🏆 Answer-writing tips

  1. Write formulas with water of crystallisation (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, CuSO₄·5H₂O): examiners check them.
  2. For "strong vs weak", mention "completely / partially ionises" and "more / fewer H⁺ ions".
  3. Draw the pH scale with 7 in the middle when comparing substances.

(b) Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O 1

CBSE 2024 · 31/1/1 · Q28 · 3 marks(a) The pH of tomato juice is about 4.6. What is its nature and what would it taste like? (b) How is a strong acid different from a weak base in terms of the ions they give in water? (c) How does acid rain affect aquatic life?

(a) Acidic (pH < 7); sour taste. 1

(b) A strong acid ionises completely, giving a large number of H⁺ (H₃O⁺) ions. A weak base ionises only partly, giving a few OH⁻ ions. 1

Section B: 2 marks each

B1Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass or copper vessels?

Curd and sour substances contain acids. 1

The acids react with copper and brass to form harmful (poisonous) compounds that spoil the food and can make us ill. 1

B2Distinguish between an alkali and a base with one example each.

A base reacts with an acid to give salt and water; an alkali is a base that dissolves in water. 1

Base (not alkali): Cu(OH)₂. Alkali: NaOH. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis. 1

B3Solution P has pH 3 and solution Q has pH 11. Which has more H⁺ ions? Which will turn red litmus blue?

P (pH 3) has more H⁺ ions; lower pH = higher H⁺ concentration. 1

Q (pH 11) is basic, so it turns red litmus blue. 1

B4How is washing soda prepared from baking soda? Give one use of washing soda.

Heat baking soda: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂, then recrystallise: Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O 1

Use: removing permanent hardness of water (or glass, soap, paper industries; cleaning). 1

B5A farmer finds his soil is too acidic. What should he add and why?

Quick lime (CaO), slaked lime (Ca(OH)₂) or chalk (CaCO₃). 1

These are basic and neutralise the excess acid, bringing the soil to the pH suitable for crops. 1

Section C: 3 marks each

C1A metal compound A reacts with dilute HCl to give effervescence. The gas extinguishes a burning candle. One product is calcium chloride. Identify A and the gas, and write the balanced equation.

A = calcium carbonate, CaCO₃. 1

Gas = carbon dioxide (it does not support burning). 1

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) 1

C2What is baking powder? How does it make cake soft and spongy? Why is tartaric acid added?

Baking powder = baking soda (NaHCO₃) + a mild edible acid such as tartaric acid. 1

When heated or wet, it gives CO₂: NaHCO₃ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O + sodium salt of the acid. The CO₂ gas makes the cake rise and become spongy. 1

Tartaric acid neutralises the sodium carbonate that would otherwise form and make the cake taste bitter. 1

C3Explain the importance of pH in (a) our digestive system (b) tooth decay (c) self-defence by animals.

(a) The stomach makes HCl for digestion; excess acid causes pain. Antacids like milk of magnesia neutralise it. 1

(b) Below pH 5.5 tooth enamel corrodes. Bacteria make acids from leftover sugar; basic toothpaste neutralises them. 1

(c) Bee stings and nettle hairs inject methanoic acid. A mild base like baking soda (or a dock leaf for nettle) gives relief. 1

C4Name the gas evolved at each electrode when brine is electrolysed. Give one use each of the three products.

Anode: Cl₂; cathode: H₂; NaOH forms in solution near the cathode. 1

H₂: fuel, margarine, ammonia. Cl₂: water treatment, PVC, bleaching powder. 1

NaOH: soaps and detergents, paper, artificial fibres, degreasing metals. 1

Section D: 5 marks

D1(a) Write the chemical name and formula of Plaster of Paris and how it is prepared. (b) Write the reaction when it is mixed with water. (c) Give two uses. (d) Why is it called a hemihydrate? (e) What happens if gypsum is heated above 373 K?

(a) Calcium sulphate hemihydrate, CaSO₄·½H₂O, made by heating gypsum at 373 K: CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O 2

(b) CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (sets into a hard mass) 1

(c) Plastering fractured bones; making toys, decorative items and smooth surfaces. 1

(d) Half a water molecule per formula unit (two units share one molecule). (e) It loses all water and becomes anhydrous CaSO₄ (dead-burnt plaster), which does not set. 1

NCERT

All textbook questions answered

In-text questions

1You have three test tubes: one has distilled water, the others an acidic and a basic solution. With only red litmus paper, how will you identify them?
Dip red litmus in each. The one that turns it blue is the basic solution. Now put this blue litmus into the other two: the one that turns it red is acidic; the one with no change is distilled water.
2Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass and copper vessels?
They contain acids that react with copper and brass to form harmful (toxic) compounds, which spoil the food and can cause food poisoning.
3Which gas is usually liberated when an acid reacts with a metal? Illustrate with an example. How will you test for it?
Hydrogen. Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂. Test: bring a burning candle or splinter near the gas (or soap bubbles filled with it): it burns with a pop sound.
4Metal compound A reacts with dilute HCl to produce effervescence. The gas evolved extinguishes a burning candle. One compound formed is calcium chloride. Write the balanced equation.
A is calcium carbonate: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l). The CO₂ puts out the candle.
5Why do HCl, HNO₃ etc. show acidic character in aqueous solutions while solutions of compounds like alcohol and glucose do not?
HCl and HNO₃ ionise in water to give H⁺ (H₃O⁺) ions, which cause acidic behaviour. Alcohol and glucose contain hydrogen but do not ionise to give H⁺ ions in water.
6Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?
Because the acid gives ions (H₃O⁺ and the negative ion) in water, and these ions carry the electric current.
7Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?
Dry HCl gas does not give H⁺ ions. H⁺ ions form only when HCl dissolves in water, so without water there is no acidic behaviour and no colour change.
8While diluting an acid, why is it recommended that the acid should be added to water and not water to the acid?
Dissolving an acid in water is highly exothermic. If water is added to concentrated acid, the heat produced can make the mixture boil and splash out, causing burns, and the glass container may crack. Adding acid slowly to a large amount of water with stirring spreads the heat safely.
9How is the concentration of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) affected when a solution of an acid is diluted?
It decreases: the same number of H₃O⁺ ions is spread over a larger volume, so there are fewer per unit volume.
10How is the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH⁻) affected when excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide?
It increases, because the extra base gives more OH⁻ ions per unit volume (up to a limit).
11You have two solutions, A and B. The pH of A is 6 and of B is 8. Which has more hydrogen ion concentration? Which is acidic and which basic?
A (pH 6) has more H⁺ ions. A is acidic; B is basic.
12What effect does the concentration of H⁺(aq) ions have on the nature of the solution?
The higher the H⁺ concentration, the more acidic the solution (lower pH). As H⁺ concentration falls, the solution becomes neutral (pH 7) and then basic.
13Do basic solutions also have H⁺(aq) ions? If yes, why are they basic?
Yes. All water solutions contain some H⁺ ions. Basic solutions are basic because their OH⁻ concentration is greater than their H⁺ concentration.
14Under what soil condition do you think a farmer would treat the soil of his fields with quick lime, slaked lime or chalk?
When the soil is too acidic. These substances are basic and neutralise the excess acidity.
15What is the common name of the compound CaOCl₂?
Bleaching powder.
16Name the substance which on treatment with chlorine yields bleaching powder.
Dry slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O.
17Name the sodium compound used for softening hard water.
Washing soda, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.
18What will happen if a solution of sodium hydrogencarbonate is heated? Give the equation.
It decomposes to give sodium carbonate, water and carbon dioxide: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.
19Write an equation to show the reaction between Plaster of Paris and water.
CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum, a hard solid mass).

Exercises

1A solution turns red litmus blue; its pH is likely to be (a) 1 (b) 4 (c) 5 (d) 10
(d) 10. It is basic, so pH > 7.
2A solution reacts with crushed egg-shells to give a gas that turns lime water milky. The solution contains (a) NaCl (b) HCl (c) LiCl (d) KCl
(b) HCl. Egg-shells are CaCO₃; acid + carbonate gives CO₂.
310 mL of a solution of NaOH is found to be completely neutralised by 8 mL of a given solution of HCl. If we take 20 mL of the same NaOH solution, the amount of HCl needed will be (a) 4 mL (b) 8 mL (c) 12 mL (d) 16 mL
(d) 16 mL. Double the base needs double the acid.
4Which type of medicine is used for treating indigestion? (a) antibiotic (b) analgesic (c) antacid (d) antiseptic
(c) antacid.
5Write word equations and then balanced equations for the reaction of: (a) dilute sulphuric acid with zinc granules (b) dilute hydrochloric acid with magnesium ribbon (c) dilute sulphuric acid with aluminium powder (d) dilute hydrochloric acid with iron filings
(a) zinc + sulphuric acid → zinc sulphate + hydrogen: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
(b) magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
(c) aluminium + sulphuric acid → aluminium sulphate + hydrogen: 2Al + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂
(d) iron + hydrochloric acid → iron(II) chloride + hydrogen: Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂
6Compounds such as alcohols and glucose also contain hydrogen but are not categorised as acids. Describe an activity to prove it.
Fix two iron nails on a cork in a beaker and connect them through a bulb and a 6 V battery. Pour dilute HCl into the beaker: the bulb glows (ions present). Repeat with glucose solution and alcohol solution: the bulb does not glow. So glucose and alcohol do not give H⁺ ions and are not acids.
7Why does distilled water not conduct electricity, whereas rain water does?
Distilled water has almost no ions. Rain water dissolves gases like CO₂ (and SO₂, NO₂) from the air, forming acids that give ions, so it conducts electricity.
8Why do acids not show acidic behaviour in the absence of water?
Acidic behaviour is due to H⁺ ions, and acids release H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) only when dissolved in water.
9Five solutions A, B, C, D and E, when tested with universal indicator, showed pH 4, 1, 11, 7 and 9. Which solution is (a) neutral (b) strongly alkaline (c) strongly acidic (d) weakly acidic (e) weakly alkaline? Arrange the pH in increasing order of hydrogen-ion concentration.
(a) D (7) (b) C (11) (c) B (1) (d) A (4) (e) E (9).
Increasing H⁺ concentration: C (11) < E (9) < D (7) < A (4) < B (1).
10Equal lengths of magnesium ribbon are taken in test tubes A and B. HCl is added to A and acetic acid to B, in the same amount and concentration. In which test tube will the fizzing occur more vigorously and why?
In A. HCl is a strong acid and ionises completely, giving many more H⁺ ions than acetic acid (a weak acid), so hydrogen is produced faster.
11Fresh milk has a pH of 6. How do you think the pH will change as it turns into curd? Explain.
The pH will decrease (below 6). Bacteria convert milk sugar into lactic acid, making curd more acidic.
12A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. (a) Why does he shift the pH of the fresh milk from 6 to slightly alkaline? (b) Why does this milk take a long time to set as curd?
(a) A slightly alkaline milk does not turn sour quickly, so it stays fresh for longer.
(b) The lactic acid formed first has to neutralise the baking soda before the milk can become acidic enough to set, so curd formation takes longer.
13Plaster of Paris should be stored in a moisture-proof container. Explain why.
PoP absorbs water and changes into gypsum, a hard solid mass: CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O. It would then be useless, so it must be kept away from moisture.
14What is a neutralisation reaction? Give two examples.
A reaction between an acid and a base that gives salt and water. Examples: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O; Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O (antacid in the stomach). Also 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O.
15Give two important uses of washing soda and baking soda.
Washing soda: (1) glass, soap and paper industries; (2) removing permanent hardness of water (also household cleaning, making borax).
Baking soda: (1) baking powder for cakes and bread; (2) antacid (also soda-acid fire extinguishers).
