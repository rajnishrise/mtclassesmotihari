📌 Chapter snapshot

  • A chemical reaction forms new substances. Signs: change in state, colour, temperature, gas given off, or a precipitate formed.
  • A chemical equation uses symbols and formulae: reactants → products. It must be balanced (same number of each atom on both sides) because mass is conserved.
  • State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq). Conditions (heat, light, catalyst) go on the arrow.
  • Five types: combination (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B: by heat, light or electricity), displacement (A + BC → AC + B), double displacement (AB + CD → AD + CB, often a precipitate), and redox.
  • Exothermic reactions give out heat (burning, respiration, CaO + water). Endothermic reactions absorb heat (most decompositions).
  • Oxidation = gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. Reduction = loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen. Both happen together in a redox reaction.
  • Corrosion (rusting of iron) and rancidity (oxidation of fats in food) are oxidation effects we see every day.
  • CBSE weightage: unit Chemical Substances (Ch 1–4) carries 25 marks. From this chapter expect 2 to 3 MCQs or an A–R, plus a 2-mark and a 3-mark question on equations, types or redox.
1.1

Chemical reactions and how we write them

When milk is left out in summer, it turns sour. When an iron pan is left wet, it gets rusty. In both cases the nature and identity of the starting substance changes and a new substance forms. Such a change is a chemical reaction. The substances that react are reactants, and the new substances formed are products.

Activity 1.1: burning magnesium ribbon

Clean a 3–4 cm magnesium ribbon with sandpaper, hold it with tongs and burn it. It burns with a dazzling white flame and leaves a white powder of magnesium oxide. (Keep the ribbon away from your eyes and burn it over a watch glass.)

1.1.1 Writing a chemical equation

A word equation like "magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide" is long. We use chemical formulae instead: Mg + O₂ → MgO. This is a skeletal chemical equation: it is not yet balanced.

⚠️ Watch out

  • Reactants go on the left, products on the right, and the arrow points to the products.
  • If there is more than one reactant or product, join them with a + sign.
  • Never change a formula (subscript) to balance. H₂O stays H₂O; you can only write 2H₂O.

🧪 Try itSpot reactions in your kitchen

  1. Watch these at home: milk turning to curd, a cut apple turning brown, LPG burning, a tablet of Eno in water, a copper vessel turning dull.
  2. For each one, write which sign of a chemical change you saw: colour, gas, temperature or a new solid.
  3. Which ones release heat? (Burning LPG and dissolving Eno are good ones to compare: one gets hot, the other fizzes.)
1.1.2

Balancing a chemical equation

The law of conservation of mass says mass can be neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. So the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides. An equation that follows this is a balanced chemical equation.

The hit-and-trial method (NCERT example: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂)

  1. Write the skeletal equation and draw a box around each formula. Do not change anything inside the boxes.
  2. Count atoms of each element on both sides: Fe 1 vs 3, H 2 vs 2, O 1 vs 4.
  3. Start with the compound with the most atoms (Fe₃O₄) and the element with the largest mismatch (oxygen). Make O equal: 4H₂O on the left. Now Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂.
  4. Balance hydrogen: 8 H on the left, so write 4H₂ on the right.
  5. Balance the last element: 3 Fe on the right, so 3Fe on the left.
  6. Check every element again: Fe 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 4 = 4. ✓
  7. Add state symbols and conditions: 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g). (Water is written as (g) because steam is used.)
ElementLeft (reactants)Right (products)Status
Fe33✓
H4 × 2 = 84 × 2 = 8✓
O4 × 1 = 44✓

🏆 Balancing tricks that save time

  • Treat a polyatomic ion that appears unchanged on both sides (SO₄, NO₃, CO₃, OH) as one unit. Example: BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → BaSO₄ + AlCl₃: count SO₄ groups, not S and O separately. Answer: 3BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → 3BaSO₄ + 2AlCl₃.
  • Balance H and O last, and lone elements (Fe, Na, O₂, H₂) at the very end.
  • If you get an odd number against a diatomic molecule (like 3 O on one side and O₂ on the other), double everything: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.

Worked example (NCERT in-text Q): Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂

H: left 2, right 1 + 2 = 3. Make it even: write 2NaOH (H = 2 + 2 = 4 on the right, O = 2). Then 2H₂O on the left (H 4, O 2). Finally 2Na. 2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)

🧪 Try itBalance with beads

  1. Take beads (or rajma and chana) of two colours: one for hydrogen, one for oxygen.
  2. Make "H₂" and "O₂" pairs and join them into "H₂O" models. How many H₂ pairs and O₂ pairs did you need so that no bead was left over?
  3. You just showed 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Repeat for N₂ + H₂ → NH₃ (answer: 1, 3, 2).

1.2

Types of chemical reactions: combination and decomposition

In a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed. Bonds between atoms break and new bonds form, giving new substances. Reactions are grouped by what happens to the reactants.

1.2.1 Combination reaction

Two or more reactants combine to form a single product: A + B → AB.

Combination reactionNote
CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heatquick lime → slaked lime; exothermic
C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)burning of coal
2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)formation of water
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)burning magnesium
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)whitewashing: slaked lime reacts with CO₂ in air over 2–3 days to give a shiny finish of calcium carbonate. (Not a combination: two products.)

Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Reactions in which heat is released along with the products are exothermic. Reactions that absorb energy are endothermic.

1.2.2 Decomposition reaction

A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products: AB → A + B. This is the opposite of combination. It needs energy, so most decomposition reactions are endothermic. The energy can be supplied as:

🔥

Heat: thermal

2FeSO₄ → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (lime from limestone, used to make cement)
2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂

⚡

Electricity: electrolytic

2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
(hydrogen : oxygen by volume = 2 : 1)

☀️

Light: photolytic

2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂
2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂
(used in black and white photography)

🤔 Is every decomposition endothermic?

Most are, because energy is needed to break bonds. But NCERT also calls the decomposition of vegetable matter into compost an exothermic process (compost pits get warm). CBSE 2025 tested exactly this as an assertion–reason question.

🧪 Try itFizz test for decomposition

  1. Heat a spoon of baking soda in a dry steel bowl (with an adult's help) and hold a cold glass plate above it.
  2. Water droplets form on the plate and the powder shrinks: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.
  3. Name the type of reaction and the form of energy you supplied. (Thermal decomposition; heat.)

1.2.3

Displacement and double displacement reactions

1.2.3 Displacement reaction

A more reactive element pushes out (displaces) a less reactive element from its compound: A + BC → AC + B.

Displacement reactionWhat you see
Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)blue → light green; brown coating on the nail
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)blue → colourless; brown copper on zinc
Pb(s) + CuCl₂(aq) → PbCl₂(aq) + Cu(s)lead displaces copper
Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)refining of silver (NCERT Q14); solution turns blue
Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fethermit reaction: aluminium displaces iron (NCERT Q2)

🔑 Reactivity order (learn from Ch 3)

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. A metal can displace any metal to its right from a salt solution. Copper cannot displace iron, so no change occurs when a copper wire is put in FeSO₄ solution.

1.2.4 Double displacement reaction

Two compounds exchange their ions: AB + CD → AD + CB. When one product is an insoluble solid, it settles down as a precipitate, so the reaction is also called a precipitation reaction.

Double displacementPrecipitate
Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)BaSO₄, white
Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)PbI₂, yellow
AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)AgCl, white (curdy)
K₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2KCl(aq)BaSO₄, white
NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)none: this is neutralisation, a double displacement without a precipitate

⚠️ Watch out

  • Displacement: an element + a compound. Double displacement: two compounds.
  • CBSE 2026 asked which pair is both double displacement and precipitation: AgNO₃ + NaCl and K₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ both qualify.
  • Write "(s)" or "↓" after the precipitate in a board answer.

🧪 Try itCoin and copper (with care)

  1. Dissolve a little blue copper sulphate (from a chemist or school lab) in water in a glass.
  2. Drop in a clean iron nail and a clean copper wire (in separate glasses). Wait 30 minutes.
  3. Which one got coated? Which solution changed colour? Explain with the reactivity series. (Iron reacts; copper does not.)
1.2.5

Oxidation and reduction (redox)

If a substance gains oxygen (or loses hydrogen) during a reaction, it is oxidised. If a substance loses oxygen (or gains hydrogen), it is reduced. One substance is oxidised while another is reduced, so the reaction is called an oxidation-reduction or redox reaction.

OxidationReduction
Oxygengain of oxygenloss of oxygen
Hydrogenloss of hydrogengain of hydrogen
The substance that causes itoxidising agent (gives oxygen or takes hydrogen; itself gets reduced)reducing agent (takes oxygen or gives hydrogen; itself gets oxidised)

More redox examples to practise

  • ZnO + C → Zn + CO: ZnO reduced, C oxidised.
  • MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂: MnO₂ loses oxygen, so it is reduced; HCl loses hydrogen, so it is oxidised (CBSE 2024).
  • 2PbO + C → 2Pb + CO₂: PbO reduced, C oxidised (NCERT Q1).
  • 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O: Na oxidised, O₂ reduced (NCERT in-text).
  • H₂S + Cl₂ → 2HCl + S: H₂S loses hydrogen (oxidised); Cl₂ gains hydrogen (reduced).

🏆 Memory trick

"OIL RIG" for oxygen and hydrogen: Oxidation Is gain of oxygen (or Loss of hydrogen), Reduction Is loss of oxygen (or Gain of hydrogen). Follow the oxygen: whoever takes it is oxidised; whoever gives it away is reduced.

🧪 Try itThe black-and-brown copper test

  1. Hold a clean copper wire (from an old cable) in a gas flame for a minute using tongs.
  2. It turns black (copper oxide). Let it cool completely.
  3. Rub one end with sandpaper: shiny brown copper is underneath, so only the surface was oxidised. Write the equation 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO and say what the copper gained. (In the lab, hot CuO + H₂ turns it brown again: reduction.)

1.3

Oxidation in daily life: corrosion and rancidity

Oxidation does not only happen in labs. It slowly spoils metals and food around us.

🔩

Corrosion

When a metal is attacked by moisture, acids or air around it. Iron gets a reddish-brown coat of rust; silver turns black; copper gets a green coat. Rusting needs both air and water (CBSE 2025 Q28). Prevention: painting, oiling, galvanising.

🍟

Rancidity

When fats and oils are oxidised, they become rancid: smell and taste change. Prevention: antioxidants, flushing chip packets with nitrogen, airtight containers, refrigeration.

🎨

Why paint iron?

Paint keeps air and moisture away from the iron surface, so rusting cannot start. It also looks good.

🏠 Rusting test (also asked in Ch 3)

Tube A: nail in tap water (air + water) → rusts. Tube B: nail in boiled water with an oil layer (no air) → no rust. Tube C: nail with anhydrous calcium chloride (dry air, no water) → no rust. Conclusion: air and water are both needed for rusting.

🧪 Try itRust race and chips test

  1. Put three iron nails in three cups: one dry, one in tap water, one in water with a layer of cooking oil on top. Check after 5 days.
  2. Open one packet of chips and keep it open for 3 days; keep another sealed. Compare the smell.
  3. Write the cause in each case (oxidation) and how it was prevented (oil layer, nitrogen gas).

⚠️ Top 8 mistakes students make

  1. Changing subscripts to balance (writing H₂O₂ instead of 2H₂O).
  2. Forgetting state symbols when the question says "with state symbols".
  3. Calling Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O a combination reaction: it has two products.
  4. Mixing up displacement (element + compound) with double displacement (two compounds).
  5. Saying the gas collected at the anode in electrolysis is hydrogen. Hydrogen is at the cathode (−) and is double in volume.
  6. Writing that the volume ratio H₂ : O₂ = 1 : 8. The volume ratio is 2 : 1; the mass ratio is 1 : 8.
  7. Naming the oxidising agent as the substance oxidised. The oxidising agent is itself reduced.
  8. Saying copper displaces iron from FeSO₄. Copper is less reactive; there is no reaction.

CBSE exam pattern and how marks are given

The CBSE Class 10 Science (086) paper is 80 marks, 3 hours, 39 questions: Section A has 20 one-mark questions (16 MCQs and 4 assertion–reason), Section B 6 two-mark, Section C 7 three-mark, Section D 3 five-mark, and Section E 3 four-mark case studies. Unit Chemical Substances: Nature and Behaviour (Chapters 1–4) carries 25 marks, the biggest unit in the paper.

✍️ How marks are given

  • Each balanced equation: usually 1 mark. An unbalanced equation gets ½. State symbols are needed only when asked.
  • Identify oxidised / reduced: ½ each, with a reason (gain or loss of oxygen) for full marks.
  • Observation questions: write what you see (colour, gas, heat), not just the product name.

🏆 Answer-writing tips

  1. Always write the type of reaction and a balanced equation, even if only one is asked: it shows understanding.
  2. Underline the precipitate or put ↓ after it; put ↑ after a gas.
  3. For redox, draw the two arcs: "loses oxygen: reduced" and "gains oxygen: oxidised".

(D). 2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂. The brown fumes are NO₂.

CBSE 2026 · 31/1/1 · Q26 · 3 marks(a) In ZnO + C → Zn + CO, identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced. (b) Balance: Pb(NO₃)₂ + KI → PbI₂ + KNO₃. (c) Write one equation each for an electrolytic and a photolytic decomposition reaction.

(a) Carbon gains oxygen: C is oxidised. Zinc oxide loses oxygen: ZnO is reduced. 1

(b) Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq) 1

(c) Electrolytic: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) (electricity). Photolytic: 2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g) (sunlight). ½ + ½

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q1 · 1 markThe ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen in water formed (or decomposed by electrolysis) is (A) 2 : 1 (B) 1 : 2 (C) 8 : 1 (D) 1 : 8

(D) 1 : 8. In H₂O, 2 × 1 = 2 g of hydrogen combines with 16 g of oxygen: 2 : 16 = 1 : 8. (By volume the gases are 2 : 1: don't mix them up.)

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q17 · 1 mark (A–R)Assertion (A): Decomposition reactions are generally endothermic. Reason (R): Decomposition of vegetable matter into compost is an exothermic reaction.

(B). Both statements are true (NCERT gives both), but R does not explain A. A is true because energy is needed to break bonds.

(1) Bubbles of a colourless gas (hydrogen) are given off. (2) The test tube becomes warm (temperature rises). ½ + ½

CBSE 2024 · 31/1/1 · Q2 · 1 markWhich of these is a photolytic decomposition (decomposition by light)? (a) 2FeSO₄ → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃ (b) CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (c) 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂ (d) 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂

Section D: 5 marks

D1(a) What is a decomposition reaction? Why is it called the opposite of combination? (b) Write one balanced equation each for decomposition by heat, light and electricity. (c) Why are most decomposition reactions endothermic?

(a) A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products. In combination, two or more reactants join into one product; decomposition does the reverse. Example: CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃ and CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. 2

(b) Heat: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Light: 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂. Electricity: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. 2

(c) Energy is needed to break the bonds in the reactant, so energy (heat, light or electricity) is absorbed. 1

In-text questions

1Why should a magnesium ribbon be cleaned before burning in air?
Magnesium reacts slowly with air on storage and gets coated with a layer of magnesium oxide. This layer stops the metal from burning easily, so it is removed by rubbing with sandpaper.
2Write the balanced equation for: (i) hydrogen + chlorine → hydrogen chloride (ii) barium chloride + aluminium sulphate → barium sulphate + aluminium chloride (iii) sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen
(i) H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl
(ii) 3BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → 3BaSO₄ + 2AlCl₃
(iii) 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
3Write a balanced chemical equation with state symbols: (i) barium chloride and sodium sulphate solutions react to give insoluble barium sulphate and sodium chloride solution. (ii) Sodium hydroxide solution reacts with hydrochloric acid solution to give sodium chloride solution and water.
(i) BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
(ii) NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
4A solution of a substance 'X' is used for whitewashing. (i) Name X and write its formula. (ii) Write the reaction of X with water.
(i) Calcium oxide (quick lime), CaO.
(ii) CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat. (On the wall, Ca(OH)₂ reacts slowly with CO₂ to form a shiny layer of CaCO₃.)
5Why is the amount of gas collected in one of the test tubes in Activity 1.7 double of the amount collected in the other? Name this gas.
Water (H₂O) contains hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2 : 1 by number of atoms, so electrolysis gives 2 volumes of hydrogen for 1 volume of oxygen: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. The gas collected in double amount is hydrogen (at the cathode).
6Why does the colour of copper sulphate solution change when an iron nail is dipped in it?
Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulphate: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu. Blue CuSO₄ is used up and light green FeSO₄ forms, so the colour changes from blue to light green.
7Give an example of a double displacement reaction other than the one given in Activity 1.10.
AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq) (white precipitate), or Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq) (yellow precipitate).
8Identify the substances that are oxidised and the substances that are reduced: (i) 4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s) (ii) CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(l)
(i) Sodium gains oxygen: Na is oxidised; O₂ is reduced.
(ii) CuO loses oxygen: CuO is reduced; H₂ gains oxygen: H₂ is oxidised.

Exercises

1Which statements about 2PbO(s) + C(s) → 2Pb(s) + CO₂(g) are incorrect? (a) Lead is getting reduced. (b) Carbon dioxide is getting oxidised. (c) Carbon is getting oxidised. (d) Lead oxide is getting reduced. Options: (i) (a) and (b) (ii) (a) and (c) (iii) (a), (b) and (c) (iv) all
(i) (a) and (b). It is lead oxide (not lead) that is reduced, and carbon (not CO₂) that is oxidised.
2Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe is an example of a (a) combination (b) double displacement (c) decomposition (d) displacement reaction
(d) displacement. Aluminium displaces iron from iron oxide (it is also a redox reaction).
3What happens when dilute hydrochloric acid is added to iron filings? (a) Hydrogen gas and iron chloride are produced. (b) Chlorine gas and iron hydroxide are produced. (c) No reaction. (d) Iron salt and water are produced.
(a). Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂
4What is a balanced chemical equation? Why should chemical equations be balanced?
An equation in which the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides is balanced. Equations must be balanced because of the law of conservation of mass: atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a reaction, so the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants.
5Translate into balanced chemical equations: (a) Hydrogen gas combines with nitrogen to form ammonia. (b) Hydrogen sulphide gas burns in air to give water and sulphur dioxide. (c) Barium chloride reacts with aluminium sulphate to give aluminium chloride and a precipitate of barium sulphate. (d) Potassium metal reacts with water to give potassium hydroxide and hydrogen gas.
(a) 3H₂(g) + N₂(g) → 2NH₃(g)
(b) 2H₂S(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) + 2SO₂(g)
(c) 3BaCl₂(aq) + Al₂(SO₄)₃(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3BaSO₄(s)
(d) 2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g)
6Balance: (a) HNO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + H₂O (b) NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O (c) NaCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl + NaNO₃ (d) BaCl₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + HCl
(a) 2HNO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O
(b) 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
(c) NaCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl + NaNO₃ (already balanced)
(d) BaCl₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2HCl
7Write the balanced chemical equations: (a) calcium hydroxide + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water (b) zinc + silver nitrate → zinc nitrate + silver (c) aluminium + copper chloride → aluminium chloride + copper (d) barium chloride + potassium sulphate → barium sulphate + potassium chloride
(a) Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O
(b) Zn + 2AgNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag
(c) 2Al + 3CuCl₂ → 2AlCl₃ + 3Cu
(d) BaCl₂ + K₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2KCl
8Write the balanced equation and identify the type: (a) potassium bromide(aq) + barium iodide(aq) → potassium iodide(aq) + barium bromide(s) (b) zinc carbonate(s) → zinc oxide(s) + carbon dioxide(g) (c) hydrogen(g) + chlorine(g) → hydrogen chloride(g) (d) magnesium(s) + hydrochloric acid(aq) → magnesium chloride(aq) + hydrogen(g)
(a) 2KBr(aq) + BaI₂(aq) → 2KI(aq) + BaBr₂(s): double displacement (precipitation)
(b) ZnCO₃(s) → ZnO(s) + CO₂(g): thermal decomposition
(c) H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g): combination
(d) Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g): displacement
9What does one mean by exothermic and endothermic reactions? Give examples.
Exothermic: heat is released with the products. Examples: burning of natural gas CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat; respiration; CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat.
Endothermic: heat is absorbed. Examples: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (on heating); 2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂; photosynthesis.
10Why is respiration considered an exothermic reaction? Explain.
During digestion, food is broken down into glucose. Glucose combines with oxygen in our cells and releases energy: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) → 6CO₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l) + energy. Since energy is given out, respiration is exothermic.
11Why are decomposition reactions called the opposite of combination reactions? Write equations.
In combination, two or more substances join to form one product; in decomposition, one substance breaks into two or more products. Example: CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃ (combination) and CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (decomposition); 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O and 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.
12Write one equation each for decomposition reactions where energy is supplied as heat, light or electricity.
Heat: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
Light: 2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)
Electricity: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
13What is the difference between displacement and double displacement reactions? Write equations.
In displacement, a more reactive element replaces a less reactive element from its compound: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. In double displacement, two compounds exchange ions: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃.
14In the refining of silver, the recovery of silver from silver nitrate solution involved displacement by copper metal. Write down the reaction.
Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)
15What do you mean by a precipitation reaction? Explain with examples.
A reaction in which an insoluble solid (precipitate) forms when two solutions are mixed. Examples: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ (white) + 2NaCl; Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂↓ (yellow) + 2KNO₃.
16Explain the following in terms of gain or loss of oxygen, with two examples each: (a) oxidation (b) reduction
(a) Oxidation is gain of oxygen: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO (copper oxidised); C + O₂ → CO₂ (carbon oxidised).
(b) Reduction is loss of oxygen: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (CuO reduced); ZnO + C → Zn + CO (ZnO reduced).
17A shiny brown-coloured element 'X' on heating in air becomes black. Name X and the black compound formed.
X is copper. The black compound is copper(II) oxide, CuO: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO.
18Why do we apply paint on iron articles?
Paint forms a layer that keeps air and moisture away from the iron, so it does not rust (corrosion is prevented).
19Oil and fat containing food items are flushed with nitrogen. Why?
Nitrogen is unreactive. Flushing the packet with nitrogen removes oxygen, so the fats and oils are not oxidised and the food does not become rancid.
20Explain: (a) corrosion (b) rancidity
(a) Corrosion: when a metal is attacked by moisture, acids or air around it, it slowly gets eaten away. Examples: rusting of iron (reddish-brown), black coating on silver, green coating on copper.
(b) Rancidity: when fats and oils in food are oxidised, their smell and taste change. It is prevented by adding antioxidants, keeping food in airtight containers or flushing packets with nitrogen.
