10.1

The human eye

The human eye is one of the most valuable and sensitive sense organs. It works like a camera: its lens system forms a real, inverted, diminished image on a light-sensitive screen called the retina. The brain then turns the image the right way up.

⚽

Size

The eyeball is nearly a sphere of diameter about 2.3 cm.

💧

Most bending at the cornea

Most of the refraction happens at the outer surface of the cornea, not in the eye lens.

🧠

Seeing is in the brain

The retina sends electrical signals through the optic nerve; the brain interprets them.

🤔 Real life: walking into a dark cinema hall

When you enter a dark hall from bright sunlight you cannot see for a while. The pupil takes some time to expand and let in more light (and the rods take time to become sensitive). Coming out into sunlight, the pupil quickly shrinks, and the bright light first feels painful.

📷 Camera

  • Lens forms the image
  • Film or sensor = screen
  • Diaphragm (aperture) controls light
  • Focus by moving the lens

👁️ Eye

  • Cornea + eye lens form the image
  • Retina = screen
  • Iris and pupil control light
  • Focus by changing the shape of the lens (accommodation)

🧪 Try it nowFind your blind spot

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no rods or cones, so no image is seen there: the blind spot.

✚
  1. Close your left eye. Look at the ✚ with your right eye, holding the screen (or printout) about 30 cm away.
  2. Slowly bring it closer while still looking at the ✚. At about 20–25 cm the red dot disappears! Its image has fallen on your blind spot.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • "The image on the retina is erect." No: it is real and inverted.
  • "The eye lens does most of the bending." No: most refraction is at the cornea; the lens does the fine adjustment.
  • "The pupil controls the size of the iris." It is the other way: the iris controls the pupil.

🌟 Did you know?

Eye donation: a donated cornea can give sight to people with corneal blindness. Eyes can be donated after death (within 4–6 hours) by anyone, with or without spectacles, even with cataract surgery. One pair of eyes can give vision to up to four people.

10.1.1

Power of accommodation

The distance between the eye lens and the retina is fixed. So to see both near and far objects clearly, the eye must change the focal length of its lens. The power of accommodation is the ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length.

Looking atCiliary musclesEye lensFocal lengthPower
Far object (sky, blackboard)RelaxedThin, less curvedLongestLeast
Near object (book)ContractedThick, more curvedShortestMost
Near point (least distance of distinct vision) = 25 cmThe minimum distance at which an object can be seen most distinctly without strain, for a normal young adult. The focal length cannot be decreased below a certain limit.
Far point = infinityThe farthest point up to which the eye can see objects clearly. A normal eye can see objects between 25 cm and infinity.

🔑 Why can't we read a book held at 10 cm?

The ciliary muscles cannot make the lens thicker than a limit. For an object closer than 25 cm, the image would form behind the retina, so it looks blurred and the eye feels strain.

Solved: how much does the eye lens change? (to understand, not for exam)

Take the lens-to-retina distance as about 2.4 cm (v = +2.4 cm).

Far object (u = ∞): 1/f = 1/2.4 ⇒ P = 100/2.4 ≈ 41.7 D.

Book at 25 cm (u = −25 cm): 1/f = 1/2.4 + 1/25 ⇒ P ≈ 41.7 + 4 = 45.7 D.

So a young eye can add about 4 dioptres by accommodation. With age this falls, and the near point moves away.

🧪 Try itMeasure your near point

  1. Hold a page with small print at arm's length and slowly bring it towards one eye (other eye closed).
  2. Stop where the letters just start to blur. Ask a friend to measure the distance from the eye with a scale. Young people get 10–25 cm; grandparents may get 50 cm or more.
  3. Now look at your finger 15 cm away, then at a far tree. Feel the slight "pull" while switching: that is your ciliary muscles working.

🤔 Real life: cataract

In old age the crystalline eye lens sometimes becomes milky and cloudy. This is cataract. It causes partial or complete loss of vision. It is corrected by cataract surgery: the cloudy lens is removed and an artificial (intra-ocular) lens is fitted.

10.2

Defects of vision and their correction

Sometimes the eye gradually loses its power of accommodation, and the person cannot see clearly. There are three common refractive defects, all corrected with suitable spherical lenses.

(a) Myopia (near-sightedness)

A myopic person can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly. The far point is nearer than infinity (only a few metres). The image of a distant object forms in front of the retina.

Causes

  1. Excessive curvature of the eye lens (focal length too short, lens too powerful).
  2. Elongation of the eyeball (retina too far from the lens).

Correction

A concave lens of suitable power. It diverges the rays so they seem to come from the far point; the eye then focuses them on the retina. Power: P = −1/(far point in m), negative.

(b) Hypermetropia (far-sightedness)

A hypermetropic person can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects distinctly. The near point is farther than 25 cm. The light rays from a close object would focus behind the retina.

Causes

  1. The focal length of the eye lens is too long (lens not curved enough).
  2. The eyeball has become too small (too short).

Correction

A convex lens of suitable power. It provides the extra converging power so the image of an object at 25 cm forms on the retina. Power is positive: use u = −25 cm, v = −(near point).

(c) Presbyopia

The power of accommodation usually decreases with ageing. The near point gradually recedes, and old people find it hard to read without glasses. This is presbyopia. It is caused by the gradual weakening of the ciliary muscles and the diminishing flexibility of the eye lens.

Correction

Usually a convex lens (for reading). Sometimes a person has both myopia and hypermetropia; then bi-focal lenses are used: the upper portion is a concave lens (distant vision) and the lower portion is a convex lens (near vision). Today these defects can also be corrected by contact lenses or surgery.

Solved: myopia (NCERT type)

The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm in front of the eye. Find the nature and power of the lens needed.

The lens must form a virtual image at the far point for an object at infinity: u = ∞, v = −80 cm. 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/80 ⇒ f = −80 cm = −0.8 m.

P = 1/f = 1/(−0.8) = −1.25 D. A concave lens.

Solved: hypermetropia (NCERT type)

The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. Find the power of the lens needed (normal near point 25 cm).

Object at 25 cm should give a virtual image at 1 m: u = −25 cm, v = −100 cm.

1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/100 + 1/25 = 3/100 ⇒ f = +33.3 cm = +0.333 m; P = +3.0 D. A convex lens.

🧠 Memory trick

MyopiA → concAve (both have "a" in the right place). Hypermetropia → convex ("hyper means more": needs more converging power). Myopic = can see near = can't see the mountain far away.

🧪 Try itThe pinhole spectacles test

  1. Make a tiny hole in a black card with a needle. If you (or a friend who wears specs) look at a distant board through it without spectacles, the letters look sharper.
  2. Reason: the pinhole lets only a narrow cone of rays through, so the blur circle on the retina becomes small. Eye doctors use this to check that a problem is a refractive defect.
  3. Ask a family member for their spectacles: a lens that makes letters smaller is concave (myopia); one that makes them bigger is convex (hypermetropia or reading glasses).

🤔 Real life: why are more students becoming myopic?

Long hours of close work on phones and books and very little time outdoors are linked with myopia in children. Eye doctors suggest the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet (6 m) away for 20 seconds, and play outdoors in daylight every day.

10.3

Refraction of light through a glass prism

A prism has two triangular bases and three rectangular lateral surfaces. The two lateral faces through which light enters and leaves are inclined to each other. The angle between them is the angle of the prism (A).

1️⃣

At the first face

Air → glass (rarer to denser): the ray bends towards the normal.

2️⃣

At the second face

Glass → air (denser to rarer): the ray bends away from the normal.

↘️

Net result

Because the faces are not parallel, the emergent ray is bent towards the base of the prism.

🔑 Angle of deviation (D)

The peculiar shape of the prism makes the emergent ray bend at an angle to the direction of the incident ray. This angle is the angle of deviation, ∠D. (Extra, for understanding: ∠D = ∠i + ∠e − ∠A.)

⚠️ Prism vs glass slab

In a rectangular glass slab the two refracting faces are parallel, so the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray (only shifted sideways): no deviation. In a prism the faces are inclined, so the ray is deviated.

🧪 Activity 10.1Trace the path of light through a prism

  1. Fix a white sheet on a drawing board. Place a glass prism on it so it rests on its triangular base, and trace its outline (PQR).
  2. Draw a line PE inclined to face AB. Fix two pins at P and Q on it.
  3. Look through face AC and fix pins R and S so they appear in a straight line with the images of P and Q.
  4. Remove the pins and prism. Join the points: you get the incident ray, refracted ray and emergent ray. Draw normals and measure ∠i, ∠r, ∠e. Extend PE forward and SF backward to meet: measure ∠D.

No prism at home? A glass of water works: a pencil looks bent and broken at the water's edge for the same reason.

10.4

Dispersion of white light by a glass prism

When a narrow beam of white light passes through a prism, it splits into a band of seven colours. The splitting of light into its component colours is called dispersion, and the band of colours is a spectrum.

🔑 Why does dispersion happen?

Different colours of light bend through different angles with respect to the incident ray as they pass through a prism. Red bends the least, violet bends the most. So the rays of each colour emerge along different paths and become distinct.

Extra (for understanding): the refractive index of glass is slightly different for each colour (more for violet, less for red), so each colour travels at a slightly different speed in glass.

VIBGYOR

most deviated (violet) ← → least deviated (red)

Newton's experiment: recombination

Isaac Newton was the first to use a glass prism to obtain the spectrum of sunlight. He tried to split the colours further with a second similar prism but could not get more colours. Then he placed a second identical prism in an inverted position: all the colours recombined and a beam of white light came out. This shows that sunlight is made up of seven colours. Any light that gives a spectrum similar to sunlight is called white light.

The rainbow: a natural spectrum

A rainbow is a natural spectrum appearing in the sky after a rain shower. It is caused by dispersion of sunlight by tiny water droplets present in the atmosphere. A rainbow is always formed in a direction opposite to the Sun: the Sun must be behind you.

🔑 Three steps in a raindrop (water drop acts like a small prism)

  1. Sunlight refracts and disperses as it enters the drop.
  2. It is reflected internally from the back of the drop.
  3. It refracts again as it comes out, and the colours reach the observer's eye at slightly different angles.

🧪 Try it at homeMake your own rainbow

  1. On a sunny morning or evening, stand with your back to the Sun and spray a fine mist of water with a hose or spray bottle: a small rainbow appears in the mist.
  2. Or put a mirror at a slant in a bowl of water near a sunny window: a spectrum appears on the wall. Under a ceiling light, tilt an old CD to see colours too.

🤔 Real life

Colours on soap bubbles, a diamond sparkling in many colours, and the coloured edges you see through a thick glass paperweight are all about light splitting into colours. A rainbow is never seen at noon in summer, because the Sun is too high.

10.5

Atmospheric refraction

The air near the Earth is not uniform. Hot air is lighter (optically rarer) than cold air above it, and air gets denser closer to the ground. Light passing through air layers of changing refractive index bends gradually: this is atmospheric refraction.

🔥 Flickering above a fire

Objects seen through the hot air rising above a fire or a hot road seem to waver or flicker. The hot air is rarer than the cooler air around it, and its refractive index keeps changing, so the apparent position of the object keeps fluctuating.

(a) Twinkling of stars

  1. Starlight entering the atmosphere is refracted continuously, bending towards the normal (rarer to denser), so the star appears slightly higher than it really is.
  2. The atmosphere is not stationary: its physical conditions keep changing. So the apparent position of the star keeps changing slightly.
  3. Stars are very far and behave like point sources. The amount of starlight entering the eye keeps changing: the star looks brighter, then fainter. This is twinkling.

🔑 Why don't planets twinkle?

Planets are much closer and look like extended sources: a planet is a collection of a large number of point sources. The changes in light from all these points average out to zero, cancelling the twinkling effect.

(b) Advance sunrise and delayed sunset

The Sun is visible to us about 2 minutes before the actual sunrise and about 2 minutes after the actual sunset. By "actual sunrise" we mean the Sun actually crossing the horizon. Light from the Sun just below the horizon bends through the atmosphere and reaches us. So the day becomes about 4 minutes longer.

🌟 Did you know?

Because of atmospheric refraction the Sun looks a little flattened (oval) at sunrise and sunset: light from its lower edge bends more than from its top edge. The same bending makes the mirage of water on a hot road in summer.

🧪 Try itSee the air shimmer

  1. Look at a distant object across the top of a burning candle, a gas stove flame or a heated tawa (ask an adult). The object seems to shiver.
  2. On a clear night, compare a bright star near the horizon with Jupiter or Venus (ask someone or use a sky app). The star twinkles strongly; the planet shines steadily.

10.6

Scattering of light

When light falls on very tiny particles (molecules of air, dust, smoke, water droplets) it is sent off in different directions. This is scattering of light. The blue sky, the colour of water in the deep sea and the reddening of the Sun at sunrise and sunset are all due to scattering.

10.6.1 Tyndall effect

The Earth's atmosphere is a heterogeneous mixture of minute particles: smoke, tiny water droplets, dust and air molecules. When a beam of light strikes them, its path becomes visible. The phenomenon of scattering of light by colloidal particles is the Tyndall effect.

🔑 Colour depends on particle size

  • Very fine particles (air molecules) scatter mainly blue light (shorter wavelengths).
  • Larger particles scatter light of longer wavelengths.
  • If the particles are large enough, the scattered light may look white (all colours scattered almost equally). That is why clouds are white.

10.6.2 Why is the colour of the clear sky blue?

The molecules of air and other fine particles are smaller than the wavelength of visible light. They scatter light of shorter wavelengths (blue end) much more strongly than longer wavelengths (red end). Red light has a wavelength about 1.8 times that of blue light.

When sunlight passes through the atmosphere, the fine particles scatter the blue colour more strongly, and this scattered blue light enters our eyes from all directions. So the sky looks blue.

🧑‍🚀 Sky of an astronaut

If the Earth had no atmosphere, there would be no scattering, and the sky would look dark. To astronauts flying at very high altitudes or on the Moon, the sky appears black even in daytime, because there is no air to scatter light.

Danger signals are red. Why?

🔑 Answer (2 marks)

Red light has the longest wavelength, so it is least scattered by fog, smoke and dust. It travels the farthest and can be seen from a long distance. Hence danger signal lights, brake lights and "stop" signs are red.

Colour of the Sun at sunrise and sunset

Near the horizon, light from the Sun passes through thicker layers of air and a larger distance in the Earth's atmosphere before reaching us. Most of the blue light and shorter wavelengths are scattered away on the way. The light that reaches our eyes is mostly of longer wavelengths, so the Sun looks reddish. At noon the Sun is overhead; its light travels a shorter distance, only a little blue is scattered, and the Sun looks white.

🧪 Activity 10.3Sunset in a beaker

  1. Dissolve about 200 g of sodium thiosulphate (hypo) in 2 L of water in a glass tank. Add 1–2 mL of concentrated sulphuric acid (only with your teacher).
  2. Shine a beam of light through the tank onto a screen. Fine sulphur particles start to form (about 2–3 minutes later).
  3. Seen from the sides, the tank looks blue (scattering of short wavelengths by tiny particles). The light reaching the screen turns orange-red, like the setting Sun. Try it in the Sky Lab below!

Safe home version: add a few drops of milk to a glass of water and shine a phone torch through it in a dark room. From the side the water looks faintly bluish; the torch looks orange through it.

🌟 Did you know?

Deep sea water looks blue partly due to scattering. After a big volcanic eruption or dust storm, sunsets become extra red for months, because the extra fine dust scatters away even more of the blue light.

⚠️ Top 8 mistakes students make

  1. Writing that ciliary muscles contract for distant objects. They relax (lens thin).
  2. Mixing up the lenses: myopia needs concave, hypermetropia needs convex.
  3. Using f in cm in P = 1/f. Convert to metres (or use P = 100/f with f in cm).
  4. For hypermetropia, taking v = −25 cm. The object is at u = −25 cm; the image is at the defective near point, v = −N.
  5. Saying the rainbow is caused by scattering. It is dispersion + refraction + internal reflection.
  6. Saying red bends most. Violet bends (deviates) most, red least.
  7. Writing "stars twinkle because they are far" only. Must mention atmospheric refraction with changing conditions, and point source.
  8. Drawing ray diagrams without arrows or without showing the far point / near point.

✍️ How the CBSE marking scheme splits marks

  • Defect of vision (3 marks): ½ for naming the defect, ½ for the reason (image in front of / behind the retina), ½ + ½ for two causes, 1 for the correction diagram (correct lens, rays with arrows, image on retina).
  • Lens power numericals: ½ for the correct u and v with signs, ½ for the formula, ½ for f, ½ for P with sign and unit (D) and the type of lens.
  • "Why" questions (twinkling, blue sky, red sun): 1 mark for the phenomenon named (atmospheric refraction / scattering) + 1 mark for the explanation in 2–3 points.
  • Prism diagram: ½ each for refracted ray, emergent ray, correct bending at both faces, and ∠D marked (with i, e, r labels).

🏆 Answer-writing tips for full marks

  1. Always name the phenomenon first: "This is due to atmospheric refraction."
  2. For defects: name → where the image forms → two causes → correcting lens → diagram. In that order.
  3. In diagrams, label O (far point) or N, N′ (near points), draw the lens in front of the eye, and put arrows on every ray.
  4. For colour questions, mention wavelength: "blue has a shorter wavelength, so it is scattered more".
  5. Write the unit D (dioptre) and the sign of power.

CBSE 2026 · 31/1/1 · Q31 · 1 markWhen you look at an object very close to your eyes: (A) ciliary muscles contract and the eye lens becomes thick (B) ciliary muscles relax and the eye lens becomes thick (C) ciliary muscles contract and the eye lens becomes thin (D) ciliary muscles relax and the eye lens becomes thin

(A). For a near object the ciliary muscles contract; the lens becomes thicker and more curved, so its focal length decreases and the image forms on the retina. This is accommodation.

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q14 · 1 markAn old person has a defect caused by weakening of the ciliary muscles and reduced flexibility of the eye lens. The defect 'a' is corrected using lens 'b'. (B) presbyopia, bifocal lens. Presbyopia comes with age; a bifocal lens (concave upper part, convex lower part) corrects both distant and near vision.

B1What is meant by power of accommodation of the eye? What happens to it with age?

The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length to see near and far objects clearly. 1

It decreases with age (ciliary muscles weaken, lens loses flexibility): presbyopia. 1

B2Why do stars twinkle? Why don't planets?

Starlight undergoes atmospheric refraction; the atmosphere's conditions keep changing, so the apparent position and the amount of light from the point-sized star keep fluctuating: twinkling. 1

Planets are nearer and act as extended sources; the fluctuations from their many points cancel. 1

Near the horizon, sunlight passes through thicker layers of air and a longer distance. ½

NCERT

All textbook questions answered

In-text questions (after section 10.2)

Q1What is meant by power of accommodation of the eye?
The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length (by the action of the ciliary muscles) so that objects at different distances are focused on the retina.
Q2A person with a myopic eye cannot see objects beyond 1.2 m distinctly. What should be the type of the corrective lens used to restore proper vision?
A concave (diverging) lens. Its focal length should be −1.2 m, so P = 1/(−1.2) = −0.83 D.
Q3What is the far point and near point of the human eye with normal vision?
Far point: infinity. Near point: about 25 cm from the eye.
Q4A student has difficulty reading the blackboard while sitting in the last row. What could be the defect the child is suffering from? How can it be corrected?
The child has myopia (near-sightedness). It is corrected with spectacles having a concave lens of suitable power.

Exercises

1The human eye can focus objects at different distances by adjusting the focal length of the eye lens. This is due to (a) presbyopia (b) accommodation (c) near-sightedness (d) far-sightedness
(b) accommodation.
2The human eye forms the image of an object at its (a) cornea (b) iris (c) pupil (d) retina
(d) retina.
3The least distance of distinct vision for a young adult with normal vision is about (a) 25 m (b) 2.5 cm (c) 25 cm (d) 2.5 m
(c) 25 cm.
4The change in focal length of an eye lens is caused by the action of the (a) pupil (b) retina (c) ciliary muscles (d) iris
(c) ciliary muscles.
5A person needs a lens of power −5.5 dioptres for correcting his distant vision. For correcting his near vision he needs a lens of power +1.5 dioptre. What is the focal length of the lens required for correcting (i) distant vision, and (ii) near vision?

(i) f = 1/P = 1/(−5.5) m = −0.182 m = −18.2 cm (concave lens).

(ii) f = 1/(+1.5) m = 0.667 m = +66.7 cm (convex lens).

6The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm in front of the eye. What is the nature and power of the lens required to correct the problem?

For an object at infinity the lens must form a virtual image at the far point: u = ∞, v = −80 cm, so f = v = −80 cm = −0.8 m.

P = 1/f = −1.25 D. A concave lens.

7Make a diagram to show how hypermetropia is corrected. The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. What is the power of the lens required to correct this defect? Assume that the near point of the normal eye is 25 cm.

u = −25 cm, v = −100 cm. 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = −1/100 + 1/25 = 3/100 ⇒ f = 33.3 cm = 0.333 m.

P = 1/f = +3.0 D. A convex lens.

8Why is a normal eye not able to see clearly the objects placed closer than 25 cm?
The focal length of the eye lens cannot be decreased below a certain minimum (the ciliary muscles can make the lens only so thick). For objects closer than 25 cm the image would form behind the retina, so the object looks blurred and the eye is strained.
9What happens to the image distance in the eye when we increase the distance of an object from the eye?
The image distance stays the same: it is the fixed distance between the eye lens and the retina. The eye lens changes its focal length (becomes thinner, focal length increases) so that the image still forms on the retina.
10Why do stars twinkle?
Starlight undergoes continuous atmospheric refraction before reaching us. Since the physical conditions of the atmosphere keep changing, the apparent position of the star keeps changing slightly, and the amount of starlight entering the eye flickers. The star is a point source, so it appears to brighten and fade: it twinkles.
11Explain why the planets do not twinkle.
Planets are much closer and appear as extended sources. A planet is a collection of many point sources; the variations in light from all of them average out to zero, so there is no twinkling.
12Why does the Sun appear reddish early in the morning?
In the morning the Sun is near the horizon, and its light passes through thicker layers of air and a larger distance. Most of the blue and shorter-wavelength light is scattered away; mainly red (longer wavelength) light reaches our eyes, so the Sun looks reddish.
13Why does the sky appear dark instead of blue to an astronaut?
At very high altitudes there is practically no atmosphere, so there are no particles to scatter sunlight. No scattered light reaches the astronaut's eyes from the sky, so it looks dark.
