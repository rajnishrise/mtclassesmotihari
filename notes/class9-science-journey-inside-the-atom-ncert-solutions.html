Questions are slightly shortened from the textbook.

1
In a model of Thomson’s atom made of clay (positive charge) and beads (electrons), what happens if (i) the positive charge of the clay is less than the total negative charge of the beads? (ii) the clay itself carries some negative charge? Would it still be a neutral atom?
Key ideaA neutral atom has total positive charge = total negative charge
  1. Step 1
    (i) Negative charge > positive charge, so the model has an overall negative charge
  2. Step 2
    (ii) The clay’s negative charge adds to the beads’ negative charge, so again negative > positive
  3. AnswerIn both cases the model is not a neutral atom
2
Is an orange or a lemon, with seeds inside soft pulp, a good comparison for Thomson’s model?
  1. Step 1
    Matches: the seeds (electrons) sit inside a soft body (positive charge) that fills the fruit
  2. Step 2
    Falls short: seeds are bunched in the centre and in segments, not spread evenly; the fruit has peel and segments; nothing in the fruit carries charge
  3. AnswerIt is a partly good comparison: it shows electrons embedded in a body, but not the even spread or the balance of charges
3
Why did Thomson conclude that electrons are present in all atoms?
  1. Step 1
    Cathode rays behaved the same whatever metal the cathode was made of
  2. Step 2
    They were also the same whatever gas filled the tube
  3. Step 3
    So the same negative particles came out of every kind of atom
  4. AnswerElectrons are a part of every atom
4
What would happen if α-particles were replaced by negatively charged particles in the gold foil experiment?
  1. Step 1
    The nucleus is positive, so negative particles would be attracted towards it, not repelled
  2. Step 2
    Most would still pass straight through the empty space
  3. Step 3
    Those passing close to a nucleus would be pulled towards it and swing round it
  4. AnswerPaths would bend towards the nucleus; none would bounce straight back
5
How does the bouncing back of a few α-particles rule out the plum pudding model?
  1. Step 1
    In Thomson’s model, positive charge and mass are spread thinly over the whole atom
  2. Step 2
    A spread-out charge can only nudge a fast, heavy α-particle a little; it cannot throw it back
  3. Step 3
    Bouncing back needs a tiny region with a lot of positive charge and mass packed together: a nucleus
  4. AnswerThomson’s model has no such dense centre, so it must be wrong
6
If you could ask Rutherford one question, what would it be?
  1. Step 1
    Any thoughtful question is correct. Examples: “Why did you choose gold?” (it can be hammered into very thin sheets)
  2. Step 2
    “How did you see where the α-particles went?” (each made a tiny flash on a zinc sulphide screen)
  3. AnswerOpen-ended: write your own question with a reason
7
Assertion (A): Rutherford concluded that most of the mass of an atom is in a small central region, the nucleus. Reason (R): In Thomson’s model, electrons are embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge.
  1. Step 1
    A is true (result of the gold foil experiment)
  2. Step 2
    R is true, but it describes Thomson’s model, so it does not explain Rutherford’s conclusion
  3. Answer(ii) Both are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
8
Name a new element after yourself and show that its symbol follows IUPAC rules.
Key ideaRules: one or two letters, first capital, second small, taken from the name, not already in use
  1. Step 1
    Example: Aarav → Aaravium, symbol Av
  2. Step 2
    First letter capital, second small; letters from the name; no element already has Av
  3. AnswerAaravium, Av (a symbol like Ar would not work: argon already uses it)
9
What problems could arise if every scientist used different symbols for the same element?
  1. Step 1
    The same symbol could mean different elements to different people
  2. Step 2
    Formulae and equations could not be shared or checked across countries
  3. Step 3
    This would cause confusion and dangerous mistakes in labs, medicine and industry
  4. AnswerA single IUPAC system avoids this confusion
10
An atom has atomic number 26 and 56 nucleons. Find its number of electrons, protons and neutrons.
GivenZ = 26, A = 56
Key ideaProtons = electrons = Z; neutrons = A − Z
  1. Step 1
    Protons = 26, electrons = 26
  2. Step 2
    Neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30
  3. Answer26 p, 26 e⁻, 30 n (iron)
11
A nucleus has 20 protons and mass number 41. How many neutrons does it have?
  1. Step 1
    Neutrons = A − Z = 41 − 20
  2. Answer21 neutrons
12
An atom has 18 neutrons and atomic number 17. Find its mass number.
  1. Step 1
    Mass number = protons + neutrons = 17 + 18
  2. Answer35
13
An atom with mass number 23 has 11 electrons. How many neutrons does it have?
  1. Step 1
    In a neutral atom, protons = electrons = 11
  2. Step 2
    Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12
  3. Answer12 neutrons
14
How many electrons are in the outermost shell of carbon-12, fluorine-19 and silicon-28?
Key ideaFill shells K = 2, L = 8, M = 8
  1. Step 1
    Carbon, Z = 6: 2, 4 → 4
  2. Step 2
    Fluorine, Z = 9: 2, 7 → 7
  3. Step 3
    Silicon, Z = 14: 2, 8, 4 → 4
  4. AnswerC: 4, F: 7, Si: 4
15
Write the electronic configuration of the elements with atomic numbers 12, 16 and 18.
  1. Step 1
    Z = 12 (magnesium): 2, 8, 2
  2. Step 2
    Z = 16 (sulfur): 2, 8, 6
  3. Step 3
    Z = 18 (argon): 2, 8, 8
  4. Answer2, 8, 2 · 2, 8, 6 · 2, 8, 8
16
Riddle: My mass number is 23, I have 11 protons, and I am a soft metal that reacts vigorously with water. Who am I, and how many neutrons do I have? Make a riddle of your own.
  1. Step 1
    11 protons → atomic number 11 → sodium
  2. Step 2
    Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12
  3. Step 3
    Own riddle: “I have 12 protons and 12 neutrons, I burn with a dazzling white light and sit at the centre of chlorophyll” (magnesium)
  4. AnswerSodium (Na), 12 neutrons
17
Two atoms each have 11 protons. One has 12 neutrons and the other 13. Compare their atomic numbers and mass numbers. How are they related?
  1. Step 1
    Atomic numbers: both 11 (same element, sodium)
  2. Step 2
    Mass numbers: 11 + 12 = 23 and 11 + 13 = 24 (different)
  3. AnswerSame Z, different A: they are isotopes
18
Bromine has two isotopes: bromine-79 (49.7%) and bromine-81 (50.3%). Find its average atomic mass.
Key ideaAverage atomic mass = Σ (mass × percentage100)
  1. Step 1
    79 × 49.7100 = 39.263
  2. Step 2
    81 × 50.3100 = 40.743
  3. Step 3
    Sum = 39.263 + 40.743 = 80.006
  4. Answer≈ 80 u
19
What is the combining capacity of nitrogen in NH3 and of magnesium in MgCl2?
  1. Step 1
    One N joins with 3 H atoms → valency 3
  2. Step 2
    One Mg joins with 2 Cl atoms → valency 2
  3. AnswerN: 3, Mg: 2
20
How are the two electrons of helium arranged?
  1. AnswerBoth are in the K shell, which is then full
21
For the lithium atom in the figure (3 protons, 4 neutrons), find the atomic number and mass number.
  1. Step 1
    Atomic number = protons = 3
  2. Step 2
    Mass number = 3 + 4 = 7
  3. AnswerZ = 3, A = 7
22
How many electrons do protium, deuterium and tritium have?
  1. Step 1
    All three are isotopes of hydrogen with 1 proton each
  2. AnswerOne electron each
23
Will atoms that already have 8 electrons in their outer shell lose or gain electrons?
  1. Step 1
    Their octet is complete, so they are stable and unreactive
  2. AnswerNeither; valency = 0 (helium, neon, argon)

Revise, Reflect, Refine

15 questions
1
Which statements about the gold foil experiment are correct? (i) It showed that neutrons are present in the nucleus. (ii) It disproved the plum pudding model and led to the idea of a central nucleus. (iii) Large deflections showed that most of the mass and positive charge are in a tiny centre. (iv) The deflections showed that electrons move around the nucleus.
  1. Step 1
    (i) Incorrect: neutrons have no charge and were found later by Chadwick (1932)
  2. Step 2
    (ii) Correct: a spread-out charge cannot turn α-particles through large angles
  3. Step 3
    (iii) Correct: only a tiny, heavy, concentrated positive centre can deflect them strongly
  4. Step 4
    (iv) Incorrect: electrons are too light to deflect α-particles
  5. AnswerCorrect: (ii) and (iii)
2
Bohr’s model: correct or incorrect? (i) Electrons lose energy in fixed orbits and fall into the nucleus. (ii) Electrons can be anywhere, with no fixed energy. (iii) Electrons revolve in orbits of fixed energy without losing energy. (iv) Electrons can be found between energy levels.
  1. Step 1
    (i) Incorrect: in a fixed orbit an electron does not lose energy
  2. Step 2
    (ii) Incorrect: electrons are only in certain shells, each of fixed energy
  3. Step 3
    (iii) Correct: this is Bohr’s main idea
  4. Step 4
    (iv) Incorrect: electrons jump between levels; they are never in between
  5. AnswerOnly (iii) is correct
3
Three nuclei: X (18 protons, 19 neutrons), Y (17 protons, 18 neutrons), Z (17 protons, 20 neutrons). What is the relation between (i) Y and Z (ii) Z and X?
  1. Step 1
    X: atomic number 18, mass number 37. Y: 17, 35. Z: 17, 37
  2. Step 2
    (i) Y and Z: same atomic number, different mass numbers
  3. Step 3
    (ii) Z and X: same mass number, different atomic numbers
  4. Answer(i) isotopes (of chlorine) (ii) isobars
4
What did Rutherford conclude about the positive part of the atom from the α-particles that bounced back or turned through large angles?
  1. Step 1
    The positive part is at the centre of the atom
  2. Step 2
    It takes up a very tiny volume
  3. Step 3
    It holds all the positive charge and almost all the mass, so it is extremely dense
  4. AnswerIt is a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus
5
Arrange these models in the order in which they were proposed, and explain: (i) Bohr (ii) Thomson (iii) Rutherford (iv) Dalton.
  1. Step 1
    Dalton: atoms are indivisible
  2. Step 2
    Thomson: after the electron was found, electrons in a positive sphere
  3. Step 3
    Rutherford: gold foil experiment showed a dense central nucleus
  4. Step 4
    Bohr: fixed orbits to explain why atoms are stable
  5. Answer(iv) → (ii) → (iii) → (i); each model fixed a problem in the one before
6
Why don’t electrons fly away from the atom?
  1. Step 1
    Electrons are negative and the nucleus is positive, so the nucleus attracts them
  2. Step 2
    This pull keeps them moving around the nucleus, like the Sun’s gravity keeps planets in orbit
  3. Step 3
    In Bohr’s model they stay in fixed shells without losing energy
  4. AnswerThe attraction of the positive nucleus holds them in their shells
7
Assertion (A): The discovery of subatomic particles helped in understanding atomic structure. Reason (R): An atom has equal numbers of electrons and protons.
  1. Step 1
    Both statements are true
  2. Step 2
    R is only one fact about atoms; it does not explain how the discoveries helped
  3. Answer(ii) Both are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
8

  3. Step 3

For the four atoms in Fig. 8.17 with electrons (a) 2, 1 (b) 2, 5 (c) 2, 8, 3 (d) 2, 7, find the name, symbol, total electrons, valence electrons, valency, protons and atomic number.
Key ideaValency = valence electrons if they are 1 to 4; = 8 − valence electrons if 5 to 7

  2. Step 2

10
Both Rutherford’s and Bohr’s models have orbiting electrons. Why did Rutherford’s fail to explain the stability of the atom while Bohr’s succeeded?
  1. Step 1
    In Rutherford’s model a revolving electron is always accelerating, so it should lose energy
  2. Step 2
    It would spiral inwards and crash into the nucleus: the atom would be unstable
  3. Step 3
    Bohr said electrons move in fixed stationary orbits where their energy stays constant; energy changes only when they jump between shells
  4. AnswerBohr’s fixed-energy orbits (no energy loss) explain stability

  2. Step 2

  3. Answer39 neutrons

15
An atom has 12 protons and 12 neutrons. If all its electrons were replaced by particles with the same charge but 500 times heavier, what happens to its (i) atomic number (ii) atomic mass (iii) mass number (iv) overall charge?
  1. Step 1
    (i) Still 12 protons, so no change: Z = 12
  2. Step 2
    (ii) An electron is about 11836 u; 500 times is about 0.27 u; 12 such particles add about 3.3 u, so mass rises from about 24 u to about 27.3 u
  3. Step 3
    (iii) Mass number counts only protons and neutrons: stays 24
  4. Step 4
    (iv) 12 positive and 12 negative charges: still neutral
  5. Answer(i) no change (ii) increases (≈ 27.3 u) (iii) no change (iv) no change
