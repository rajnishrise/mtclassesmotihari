9.0

What is light? How does it travel?

We see an object when light coming from it (its own light, or light it reflects) enters our eyes. A book in a dark room is invisible: no light, no sight.

➡️

Travels in straight lines

An opaque object makes a sharp shadow. This is called rectilinear propagation of light.

⚡

Fastest thing known

Speed in vacuum c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. It slows down in water and glass.

🔁

Reflects and refracts

At a surface it can bounce back (reflection) or bend while entering (refraction).

🧪 Try it at homePinhole and shadow test

  1. Make a pinhole in a card and look at a bulb through it. Now look through a bent straw: you can't see the bulb.
  2. Hold your hand near a torch and a wall: sharp shadow. This proves light travels in straight lines.

Note: when light passes a very small opening, it bends around it slightly (diffraction). For this chapter, treat light as straight rays.

🤔 Real life

Solar eclipse, shadows of trees at noon, and the narrow beam from a torch in fog all show light moving in straight lines.

9.1

Reflection of light

When light falls on a polished surface (like a mirror) and bounces back into the same medium, it is called reflection of light. A highly polished surface such as a mirror reflects most of the light falling on it.

Law 1: Angle of incidence = Angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r)Both angles are measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface.
Law 2: Incident ray, normal and reflected ray all lie in the same plane

These laws work for all reflecting surfaces, plane or curved (spherical).

🔦 Lab 1: Laws of reflection

Drag the slider to change the angle of incidence. Watch the reflected ray.

Image in a plane mirror

  • 🟢 Image is virtual and erect (cannot be taken on a screen).
  • 📏 Image size = object size (m = +1).
  • ↔️ Image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
  • 🔄 Image is laterally inverted: your right hand looks like the left hand.

🧪 Try itAMBULANCE puzzle

Write AMBULANCE on paper and hold it in front of a mirror. Now look at an ambulance: it is painted ƎƆИA⅃UBMA so drivers ahead read it correctly in their rear-view mirror. That is lateral inversion at work.

9.2

Spherical mirrors

A mirror whose reflecting surface is part of a hollow sphere is a spherical mirror. Look at a shiny steel spoon: the inside curves in (like a cave), the back bulges out.

Concave mirror (converging)

Reflecting surface curved inwards, towards the centre of the sphere. It brings parallel rays together at a point.

Convex mirror (diverging)

Reflecting surface curved outwards. It spreads parallel rays apart, as if they came from a point behind it.

TermMeaning
Pole (P)The centre of the reflecting surface of the mirror.
Centre of curvature (C)Centre of the sphere of which the mirror is a part. In front for concave, behind for convex. It is not on the mirror.
Radius of curvature (R)Radius of that sphere; distance PC.
Principal axisStraight line through P and C. It is normal to the mirror at the pole.
Principal focus (F)Concave: the point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the axis meet after reflection. Convex: the point from which they appear to diverge.
Focal length (f)Distance PF between the pole and the principal focus.
ApertureDiameter of the reflecting surface (the width of the mirror).
R = 2f, i.e. f = R/2Valid for spherical mirrors of small aperture. F lies midway between P and C.

🧪 NCERT ActivitySpoon mirror and burning paper

  1. Spoon: look at your face in the inner side of a steel spoon (concave): far away it is upside down; very close it is erect and large. On the back (convex) it is always erect and small.
  2. Sun focus: hold a concave mirror facing the Sun and bring a sheet of paper in front of it. Find the bright, sharp spot. Keep it there for a minute: the paper starts smoking. The distance from the mirror to the spot is roughly the focal length.

⚠️ Never look at the Sun or its reflection directly. Do this only with a teacher or parent.

9.2.2

Rules for drawing ray diagrams (mirrors)

To locate an image, take any two of these four rays from the top of the object. Where the reflected rays meet (or appear to meet) is the image. Red = incident ray, blue = reflected ray.

🏆 Board tip

Use arrows on every ray, draw virtual rays and virtual images dotted, and label P, F, C, object AB and image A′B′. Missing arrows cost ½ mark in the CBSE marking scheme.

9.2.1

Image formation by a concave mirror

The nature, position and size of the image depend on where the object is. This is the single most-asked table of the chapter (NCERT Table 9.1). Learn it with the six diagrams below.

Position of objectPosition of imageSize of imageNature
At infinityAt the focus FHighly diminished, point-sizedReal and inverted
Beyond CBetween F and CDiminishedReal and inverted
At CAt CSame sizeReal and inverted
Between C and FBeyond CEnlargedReal and inverted
At FAt infinityHighly enlargedReal and inverted
Between P and FBehind the mirrorEnlargedVirtual and erect

🔑 Memory trick: "the closer, the bigger, until it flips"

As the object comes from infinity towards F, the real image moves from F out to infinity and keeps growing. Once the object crosses F, the image jumps behind the mirror and becomes virtual and erect. A concave mirror is the only mirror that can give a real image, and the only one that gives an enlarged image.

Uses of concave mirrors (real life)

🔦Torches, headlights, searchlights

Bulb at F → a powerful parallel beam.

🪒Shaving and make-up mirrors

Face between P and F → enlarged, erect image.

🦷Dentist's mirror

Enlarged image of the teeth.

☀️Solar furnaces and cookers

Large concave mirrors concentrate sunlight at F to make heat.

🧪 NCERT ActivityCatch an image on a wall

  1. Stand near a window with a concave mirror (or a steel bowl). Point it at a far tree; move a white card in front of it until you see a sharp, small, upside-down tree. That is Case 1/2.
  2. In a dark room, put a candle on a scale in front of the mirror. Move it closer step by step (beyond C, at C, between C and F, at F, inside F) and find the image on a screen each time. Record your results in a table like the one above. Inside F you get no image on the screen: look into the mirror to see the big erect image.
9.2.2

Image formation by a convex mirror

A convex mirror has only two cases (NCERT Table 9.2). The image is always virtual, erect and diminished, and it is always behind the mirror between P and F.

Position of objectPosition of imageSizeNature
At infinityAt F, behind the mirrorHighly diminished, point-sizedVirtual and erect
Between infinity and PBetween P and F, behind the mirrorDiminishedVirtual and erect

Uses of convex mirrors

🚗Rear-view (wing) mirrors of vehicles

Always erect image, and a much wider field of view because the mirror bulges outwards.

🏪Shops, ATMs, lifts

Security mirrors in corners show a large area at once.

⛰️Sharp turns on hill roads

Big convex mirrors show vehicles coming round the bend.

⚠️ "Objects in mirror are closer than they appear"

Because the image in a convex mirror is diminished, cars behind you look smaller and so farther away than they really are. That is why this warning is printed on many side mirrors.

🧪 Try itHow much can you see?

Look at the road behind you in a plane mirror, then in the back of a big steel spoon or a car's wing mirror. Count the objects you can see in each. The convex mirror shows more, even though each is smaller.

🧩 How to identify a mirror (CBSE favourite)

  • Image always erect and same size, whatever the distance → plane mirror.
  • Image always erect and smaller → convex mirror.
  • Image enlarged and erect when near, inverted when far → concave mirror.

9.2.3

Sign convention, mirror formula and magnification

New Cartesian sign convention

  1. The object is always placed to the left of the mirror, so light falls on the mirror from the left.
  2. All distances are measured from the pole (optical centre for lenses).
  3. Distances in the direction of incident light (to the right) are +; against it (to the left) are −.
  4. Heights measured upwards from the principal axis are +; downwards are −.
QuantityConcave mirrorConvex mirror
u (object distance)−−
f and R− (F in front)+ (F behind)
v (image distance)− real, + virtual+ always
m− real, + virtual+ (and less than 1)

Mirror formula

1/v + 1/u = 1/fu = object distance, v = image distance, f = focal length, all measured from P with signs. Valid for all spherical mirrors and all object positions.

Magnification

m = h′/h = −v/uh = object height (+), h′ = image height. m is negative → real and inverted. m is positive → virtual and erect. |m| > 1 → enlarged, |m| < 1 → diminished, |m| = 1 → same size.

Solved examples (NCERT)

Example 9.1: rear-view mirror

A convex mirror used as a rear-view mirror has R = 3.00 m. A bus is 5.00 m from the mirror. Find the position, nature and size of the image.

R = +3.00 m, so f = R/2 = +1.50 m; u = −5.00 m.

1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/1.50 + 1/5.00 = (10 + 3)/15 = 13/15, so v = +1.15 m (behind the mirror).

m = −v/u = −1.15/(−5.00) = +0.23.

Image: 1.15 m behind the mirror, virtual, erect, 0.23 times the size of the bus.

Example 9.2: concave mirror and screen

A 4.0 cm tall object is placed 25.0 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15.0 cm. Where should a screen be placed to get a sharp image? Find the nature and size of the image.

u = −25.0 cm, f = −15.0 cm, h = +4.0 cm.

1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/15 + 1/25 = (−5 + 3)/75 = −2/75, so v = −37.5 cm.

m = −v/u = −(−37.5)/(−25.0) = −1.5; h′ = m × h = −1.5 × 4.0 = −6.0 cm.

Screen 37.5 cm in front of the mirror. Image real, inverted, enlarged, 6.0 cm tall.

🧮 Lab 3: Mirror and lens formula solver

Pick mirror or lens, type any two of u, v, f (with signs, in cm). It finds the third, m and the nature, with full working like a board answer.

Leave exactly one of u, v, f empty. Tip: concave mirror and concave lens have negative f.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that u is always negative.
  • Writing f = +15 cm for a concave mirror. Concave mirror f is negative; convex mirror f is positive.
  • Using m = v/u for a mirror. For mirrors m = −v/u; for lenses m = +v/u.
  • Stopping at 1/v. Always invert to get v, and write the unit.
9.3

Refraction of light

Light travels in a straight line in one medium, but when it goes obliquely from one transparent medium to another, its direction changes at the boundary. This bending is called refraction of light. It happens because light has different speeds in different media.

🧪 NCERT ActivityThe disappearing coin

  1. Put a coin at the bottom of an empty steel bowl. Step back slowly until the coin just disappears behind the rim.
  2. Keep your eye there. Ask a friend to pour water gently into the bowl, without moving the coin.
  3. The coin comes back into view! Light from the coin bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, and reaches your eye over the rim.

Refraction through a rectangular glass slab

↘️

Rarer → denser

Light slows down and bends towards the normal (r < i).

↗️

Denser → rarer

Light speeds up and bends away from the normal (r > i).

⬇️

Along the normal

A ray falling normally (i = 0) goes straight, without bending.

🧪 NCERT ActivityTracing the path through a glass slab (pins)

  1. Place a glass slab on white paper fixed on a board and draw its outline ABCD.
  2. Draw an oblique line to AB, fix two pins E and F on it.
  3. Look through the opposite face CD and fix pins G and H so that they appear in line with the images of E and F.
  4. Remove the slab, join H, G to meet CD, and join to F on AB. Draw normals and measure: ∠i, ∠r and ∠e. You will find ∠e = ∠i, and EF ∥ GH.

Laws of refraction

Law 1: Incident ray, refracted ray and normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
Law 2 (Snell's law): sin i / sin r = constantfor a given pair of media and a given colour (wavelength) of light. This constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first.

🌊 Lab 4: Snell's law and glass slab

Pick a medium and change the angle of incidence. Watch how much the ray bends.

9.3.2

Refractive index

The extent of bending depends on the refractive index, which compares the speed of light in the two media.

n₂₁ = v₁ / v₂ = (speed in medium 1) / (speed in medium 2)Refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1. Also n₁₂ = v₂/v₁, so n₁₂ = 1/n₂₁.
Absolute refractive index: n = c / vc = speed of light in vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s), v = speed in the medium. Air ≈ vacuum (n = 1.0003). It has no unit.

🔑 Optical density

A medium with larger n is optically denser: light is slower in it. Optical density is not mass density. Kerosene (n = 1.44) is optically denser than water (n = 1.33), yet it is lighter and floats on water.

Solved: speed of light in glass

Light enters glass of refractive index 1.50 from air. Find its speed in glass.

n = c/v ⇒ v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/1.50 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s.

Solved: relative refractive index

n of glass = 1.5, n of water = 1.33. Find the refractive index of glass with respect to water.

ngw = ng/nw = 1.5/1.33 = 1.13. (It is more than 1, so glass is optically denser than water.)

🧪 Try itSpeed race in a glass

Pour water and then kerosene or cooking oil slowly into a clear glass so they form two layers. Shine a laser pointer or torch slit at an angle from the top: the beam bends at each boundary, more in the medium with higher n. Diamond sparkles so much because its n (2.42) is very high.

9.3.3

Refraction by spherical lenses

A transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both are spherical, is a lens.

Convex lens (converging)

Thicker at the middle, thin at the edges. Bends parallel rays together. Also called a double convex lens.

Concave lens (diverging)

Thinner at the middle, thick at the edges. Spreads parallel rays apart. Also called a double concave lens.

TermMeaning
Centre of curvature (C₁, C₂)Centres of the two spheres whose parts form the two surfaces.
Principal axisLine passing through both centres of curvature.
Optical centre (O)Central point of the lens; a ray through O passes without deviation.
Principal focusConvex: point where rays parallel to the axis converge after refraction. Concave: point from which they appear to diverge. A lens has two foci, F₁ and F₂.
Focal length (f)Distance between the optical centre and the principal focus.

Rules for ray diagrams (lenses)

🧪 NCERT ActivityBurn paper with sunlight (convex lens)

Hold a convex lens (a magnifying glass) in sunlight and focus the light on a sheet of paper. Adjust till you get a sharp bright spot and hold it for a few minutes. The paper starts burning. The spot is the image of the Sun at the focus, and the distance from lens to paper is the focal length.

⚠️ Never look at the Sun through a lens. Do it under supervision, on a stone floor, away from anything that can catch fire.

Just like the concave mirror, a convex lens gives six cases (NCERT Table 9.4). The difference: for a lens the real image is formed on the other side of the lens.

🧪 NCERT ActivityCandle on a metre scale

  1. Fix a convex lens on a stand at the 50 cm mark of a metre scale. Find its focal length by focusing a far object (a window) on a screen.
  2. Mark F₁, 2F₁ on one side and F₂, 2F₂ on the other.
  3. Place a burning candle far beyond 2F₁, then at 2F₁, between F₁ and 2F₁, at F₁, and between F₁ and O. Each time, move the screen on the other side to catch a sharp image and note its position, size and nature.
  4. When the candle is between F₁ and O, no screen image forms: look through the lens from the other side to see a large erect flame.
📷Camera, eye

Object far away → small real image (Case 2).

📽️Projector

Slide between F₁ and 2F₁ → large real image on a screen (Case 4).

🔍Magnifying glass, reading lens

Object inside F → enlarged virtual image (Case 6).

👓Spectacles for far-sighted people

Convex lenses help focus near objects.

9.3.4

Image formation by a concave lens

A concave lens always gives a virtual, erect and diminished image, between the optical centre and F₁, on the same side as the object (NCERT Table 9.5).

Position of objectPosition of imageSizeNature
At infinityAt focus F₁Highly diminished, point-sizedVirtual and erect
Between infinity and OBetween F₁ and ODiminishedVirtual and erect
🚪Door peephole (viewer)

Gives a small, erect, wide view of the person outside.

👓Spectacles for short-sighted people

Concave lenses spread the light to correct myopia.

🔭Galilean telescopes, torches

Used with convex lenses to control the beam.

🧪 Try itLens detective by touch and sight

Hold an unknown lens over printed text and move it up slowly. If the letters look bigger and erect close up (and turn upside down farther away) it is convex. If the letters always look smaller and erect, it is concave. Feel it too: thick middle = convex, thin middle = concave.

9.3.7

Lens formula and magnification

The same new Cartesian sign convention is used for lenses, with all distances measured from the optical centre O. So: u is always −; f is + for convex, − for concave; v is + for a real image (other side) and − for a virtual image (same side).

1/v − 1/u = 1/fLens formula. Note the MINUS sign (the mirror formula has a plus).
m = h′/h = v/uFor a lens, m = +v/u (no minus sign). m − → real, inverted; m + → virtual, erect.
MirrorLens
Formula1/v + 1/u = 1/f1/v − 1/u = 1/f
Magnificationm = −v/um = v/u
Real image has vnegative (in front)positive (behind the lens)
Converging one has fconcave: −convex: +

Example 9.3: concave lens

A concave lens has focal length 15 cm. At what distance should the object be placed so that it forms an image 10 cm from the lens? Find the magnification.

Concave lens image is virtual, on the same side: v = −10 cm, f = −15 cm.

1/u = 1/v − 1/f = −1/10 + 1/15 = (−3 + 2)/30 = −1/30, so u = −30 cm.

m = v/u = (−10)/(−30) = +0.33: virtual, erect, one-third the size.

Example 9.4: convex lens

A 2.0 cm tall object is placed perpendicular to the principal axis of a convex lens of focal length 10 cm, at 15 cm from the lens. Find the nature, position and size of the image, and the magnification.

h = +2.0 cm, f = +10 cm, u = −15 cm.

1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 − 1/15 = (3 − 2)/30 = 1/30, so v = +30 cm.

m = v/u = 30/(−15) = −2; h′ = m × h = −2 × 2.0 = −4.0 cm.

Image 30 cm on the other side of the lens, real, inverted, twice as large (4.0 cm).

🔑 Case-study shortcut (asked in 2026)

If a convex lens forms a real image of the same size, the object and image are both at 2f: u = −2f and v = +2f. If m = −2, then v = −2u (twice as far on the other side).

9.3.8

Power of a lens

The ability of a lens to converge or diverge light is its power. A lens of short focal length bends light more, so it has more power.

P = 1/f (f in metres)SI unit: dioptre (D). 1 D = power of a lens whose focal length is 1 metre (1 D = 1 m⁻¹).
P = 100/f (f in cm)
➕

Convex lens

f positive → power positive. e.g. f = +50 cm → P = +2 D.

➖

Concave lens

f negative → power negative. e.g. f = −25 cm → P = −4 D.

🔗

Lenses in contact

P = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + … Opticians use this to make the exact power from standard lenses.

👓 Lab 6: Power and combination calculator

Type focal lengths in cm (− for concave). Leave lens 2 empty for one lens.

Solved (CBSE 2026 case study)

A convex lens of focal length 30 cm and a concave lens of focal length 15 cm are kept in contact. Find the power and focal length of the combination.

P₁ = 100/30 = +3.33 D; P₂ = 100/(−15) = −6.67 D.

P = P₁ + P₂ = −3.33 D; f = 100/P = −30 cm. The combination behaves like a concave (diverging) lens.

🧪 Try itRead a spectacle prescription

Ask someone at home who wears glasses for their prescription card. You will see numbers like −1.50 or +2.00 under "SPH". These are powers in dioptres. Negative → concave lens (for short sight); positive → convex lens (for long sight or reading). Can you find each focal length? (−1.50 D → f = −0.67 m.)

✍️ How the CBSE marking scheme splits marks

  • Numericals (2–3 marks): ½ for writing the correct signs of u, f (or v), ½ to 1 for the formula and substitution, ½ for the answer with sign and unit, and ½ for the nature when asked.
  • Ray diagrams: about 1 mark per correct diagram: ½ for correct rays with arrows, ½ for the correct image position and labelling. A diagram without arrows usually loses ½.
  • Definitions and laws: 1 mark each, only for the complete statement (e.g. Snell's law must say "for a given pair of media and given colour of light").
  • Case studies: (i) and (ii) 1 mark each with a one-line reason; (iii) 2 marks: 1 method + 1 answer.

🏆 Answer-writing tips for full marks

  1. Start every numerical with "Given: u = …, f = …, (using new Cartesian sign convention)".
  2. Write the formula first, then substitute, then the answer with sign and unit (cm, m, D).
  3. Interpret the sign: "v is negative, so the image is real, in front of the mirror".
  4. Draw ray diagrams with a pencil and scale, arrows on all rays, dotted virtual rays, label P/O, F, C or 2F.
  5. For "nature, position and size", write all three, every time.

CBSE 2026 · 31/1/1 · Q30 · 1 markA convex lens of focal length 15 cm forms a real image whose size is equal to the size of the object. The positions of the object and the image are respectively: (A) −15 cm and −15 cm (B) −15 cm and +15 cm (C) −30 cm and +30 cm (D) −30 cm and −30 cm

(C) −30 cm and +30 cm. A same-size real image is formed only when the object is at 2F₁: u = −2f = −30 cm, and the image is at 2F₂: v = +30 cm (other side, so positive).

CBSE 2026 · 31/1/1 · Q33 · 2 marks (figure described)The figure shows the formation of a real image by a lens. (1) What type of lens is used? (2) If the real image is formed at 30 cm from the lens and is twice the size of the object, where was the object placed?

(1) Only a convex lens forms a real image. ½

(2) Real image ⇒ inverted, so m = −2; v = +30 cm. ½

m = v/u ⇒ u = v/m = 30/(−2) = −15 cm. 1

The object was placed 15 cm in front of the lens (between F₁ and 2F₁, since f = 10 cm from 1/f = 1/30 + 1/15).

CBSE 2026 · 31/1/1 · Q38 · 4 marks (case study)Passage on images by convex and concave lenses and power of lenses in contact. (a) An object is placed 40 cm from a convex lens of focal length 20 cm. Find the position and nature of the image. (b) Draw a ray diagram for an object placed between the optical centre and F of a concave lens. (c)(i) A convex lens of focal length 30 cm and a concave lens of focal length 15 cm are in contact. Find the focal length and power of the combination. OR (c)(ii) A concave lens of focal length 2 m and a convex lens of focal length 1.5 m are in contact. Will the combination behave as convex or concave? Give reason.

(a) u = −40 cm = −2f, so the object is at 2F₁. 1/v = 1/20 − 1/40 = 1/40 ⇒ v = +40 cm: at 2F₂, real, inverted, same size. 1

(b) Image between O and F₁ on the same side, virtual, erect, diminished (diagram above). 1

(c)(i) P₁ = 100/30 = +3.33 D, P₂ = 100/(−15) = −6.67 D ½; P = P₁ + P₂ = −3.33 D ½; f = 1/P = −0.30 m = −30 cm 1.

(c)(ii) P = 1/(−2) + 1/1.5 = −0.5 + 0.67 = +0.17 D 1. Net power is positive, so the combination behaves as a convex (converging) lens. 1

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q13 · 1 markMirror X is used to concentrate sunlight in a solar furnace; mirror Y is fitted on the side of a vehicle to see traffic behind. Which statements are true? (i) Image by X is real, diminished, at its focus. (ii) Image by Y is virtual, diminished, erect. (iii) Image by X is virtual, diminished, erect. (iv) Image by Y is real, diminished, at its focus. (A) (i) and (ii) (B) (ii) and (iii) (C) (iii) and (iv) (D) (i) and (iv)

(A) (i) and (ii). X is a concave mirror: the Sun is at infinity, so the image is real, point-sized, at F. Y is a convex mirror: always virtual, erect, diminished.

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q24 · 2 marksAn object is placed at a distance of 60 cm from a concave lens of focal length 30 cm. Use the lens formula to find the position of the image.

u = −60 cm, f = −30 cm (concave lens). ½

1/v − 1/u = 1/f ⇒ 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = −1/30 − 1/60 = −3/60 1

v = −20 cm: the image is 20 cm from the lens on the same side as the object (virtual, erect, diminished, m = +1/3). ½

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q36(A) · 5 marks (reworded)(a) A lens X has power −2.5 D. Name the lens and find its focal length in cm. (b) A lens forms an image with magnification −2 when the object is 20 cm from its optical centre. State the nature and size of the image and find its position. (c) Lenses A and B have focal lengths 10 cm and 20 cm. Which one has the higher degree of convergence or divergence? Why?

(a) Negative power ⇒ concave lens. f = 1/P = 1/(−2.5) m = −0.4 m = −40 cm. 1 (It is used to correct myopia.)

(b) m is negative ⇒ real and inverted; |m| = 2 ⇒ twice the size of the object. 1 u = −20 cm, m = v/u ⇒ v = m × u = (−2)(−20) = +40 cm, on the other side of the lens (it is a convex lens, f = 13.3 cm, so the image is beyond 2F₂). 1

(c) Lens A. P = 1/f; A has the shorter focal length, so its power (10 D) is greater than B's (5 D). More power means more bending. 2

CBSE 2025 · 31/1/1 · Q36(B) · 5 marks (OR, reworded)(a) Draw a ray diagram to show the path of a ray of light entering a rectangular glass slab obliquely from air and emerging out. (b) State Snell's law of refraction. (c) Distinguish between the virtual images formed by a convex lens and a concave lens on the basis of object distance and magnification.

(a) The ray bends towards the normal on entering glass and away from it on leaving; the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced. 2

(b) The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant, sin i / sin r = constant, for a given pair of media and for light of a given colour. 1

(c) Convex lens: virtual image only when the object is between O and F₁; it is magnified (m > 1). Concave lens: virtual image for any object distance; it is always diminished (m < 1). 2

(c) At twice the focal length. Object at 2F₁ gives a real, inverted, same-size image at 2F₂ (also NCERT Exercise Q3).

CBSE 2024 · 31/1/1 · Q24 · 2 marksAn object is placed at a distance of 10 cm from a convex mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the position of the image formed by the mirror.

u = −10 cm, f = +15 cm (convex mirror). ½

1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/15 + 1/10 = (2 + 3)/30 = 5/30 1

v = +6 cm: 6 cm behind the mirror, virtual and erect (m = −v/u = +0.6, diminished). ½

CBSE 2024 · 31/1/1 · Q39 · 4 marks (case study, reworded)A student studies images formed by concave mirrors. Case 1: f = 20 cm, object at 45 cm. Case 2: f = 15 cm, object at 30 cm. Mirror C: f = 30 cm, object at 20 cm. (i) In which case is the image diminished? Justify. (ii) List two properties of the image in case 2. (iii)(A) What is the nature and size of the image formed by mirror C? Draw a ray diagram. OR (iii)(B) An object is placed 18 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 12 cm. Find the position of the image.

(i) Case 1. R = 2f = 40 cm and the object at 45 cm is beyond C, so the image is real, inverted, diminished (between F and C). 1

(ii) R = 30 cm, object at 30 cm = at C: image is real and inverted, and same size as the object (at C). 1

(iii)(A) Object (20 cm) is between P and F (30 cm): image is virtual, erect and enlarged, behind the mirror (v = +60 cm, m = +3). 2

(iii)(B) u = −18 cm, f = −12 cm. 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/12 + 1/18 = (−3 + 2)/36 = −1/36 1 ⇒ v = −36 cm: 36 cm in front of the mirror, real, inverted, enlarged (m = −2). 1

Section A: MCQs (1 mark each)

A1The radius of curvature of a spherical mirror is 20 cm. Its focal length is (a) 40 cm (b) 20 cm (c) 10 cm (d) 5 cm
(c) 10 cm. f = R/2.
A2The image formed by a concave mirror is real, inverted and of the same size as the object. The object is placed (a) at F (b) at C (c) between F and C (d) beyond C
(b) at C.
A3A ray of light passes from water to air. It (a) bends towards the normal (b) bends away from the normal (c) goes undeviated (d) is reflected back
(b) Denser to rarer: speed increases, bends away from the normal.
A4The power of a lens is +4 D. Its focal length and nature are (a) 25 cm, concave (b) 25 cm, convex (c) 4 m, convex (d) 0.4 m, concave
(b) f = 100/4 = 25 cm; positive power = convex.
A5The magnification produced by a mirror is −0.5. The mirror is (a) plane (b) convex (c) concave (d) any of these
(c) concave. Negative m means a real image; only a concave mirror forms real images.
A6The refractive index of glass is 1.5. The speed of light in glass is (a) 1.5 × 10⁸ m/s (b) 2 × 10⁸ m/s (c) 3 × 10⁸ m/s (d) 4.5 × 10⁸ m/s
(b) v = c/n = 3 × 10⁸/1.5.
A7Which of these gives a virtual, erect and diminished image for every position of the object? (a) concave mirror (b) convex lens (c) convex mirror (d) plane mirror
(c) convex mirror (a concave lens also does).
A8A child sees a large, erect image of a coin in a convex lens. The coin must be (a) beyond 2F₁ (b) at 2F₁ (c) at F₁ (d) between F₁ and O
(d) Magnifying-glass case.

Section A: Assertion–Reason (1 mark each)

(a) Both A and R are true and R explains A. (b) Both true, R does not explain A. (c) A true, R false. (d) A false, R true.

AR1A: A convex mirror is used as a rear-view mirror in vehicles. R: A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image and gives a wider field of view.
(a).
AR2A: Kerosene is optically denser than water. R: Kerosene is lighter than water.
(b). Both true, but optical density depends on refractive index (1.44 > 1.33), not on mass density.
AR3A: A ray of light passing through the optical centre of a lens goes undeviated. R: Near the optical centre the two faces of the lens are almost parallel, like a thin glass slab.
(a).
AR4A: The emergent ray from a glass slab is parallel to the incident ray. R: The angle of refraction at the first face equals the angle of emergence.
(c). A is true; R is false. The angle of emergence equals the angle of incidence, not the angle of refraction.

Section B: 2 marks

B1State the two laws of reflection. Will they hold for spherical mirrors?

(i) ∠i = ∠r. (ii) Incident ray, normal and reflected ray lie in the same plane. 1½ Yes, they are valid for all reflecting surfaces, including spherical mirrors. ½

B2A concave mirror produces a three times magnified real image of an object placed 10 cm in front of it. Where is the image located?

Real ⇒ m = −3, u = −10 cm. ½ m = −v/u ⇒ −3 = −v/(−10) ⇒ v = −30 cm. 1 Image 30 cm in front of the mirror. ½

B3Light enters from air to diamond (n = 2.42). Find the speed of light in diamond. What does "refractive index of diamond is 2.42" mean?

v = c/n = 3 × 10⁸/2.42 = 1.24 × 10⁸ m/s. 1 It means the speed of light in air is 2.42 times its speed in diamond. 1

B4Why does a ray of light bend when it travels from one medium to another? In which case does it not bend?

Because the speed of light is different in different media. 1 It does not bend when it falls along the normal (i = 0). 1

B5The power of a lens is −2.0 D. Find its focal length and name the lens. A doctor prescribes +1.5 D. What lens is it?

f = 1/(−2) = −0.5 m: concave lens. 1 +1.5 D ⇒ f = +0.67 m: convex (converging) lens. 1

Section C: 3 marks

C1An object 5 cm tall is placed 25 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the position, size and nature of the image.

u = −25, f = +10, h = 5. ½ 1/v = 1/10 − 1/25 = 3/50 ⇒ v = +16.7 cm. 1 m = v/u = 16.7/(−25) = −0.67; h′ = −3.3 cm. 1 Real, inverted, diminished, 16.7 cm behind the lens. ½

C2Draw ray diagrams for a concave mirror when the object is (i) between F and C (ii) between P and F. State one use of each case.

(i) Image beyond C, real, inverted, enlarged. Use: in projectors with mirrors / to get a large image on a screen. 1½ (ii) Image behind the mirror, virtual, erect, enlarged. Use: shaving/make-up mirror, dentist's mirror. 1½

C3Refractive indices: water 1.33, kerosene 1.44, turpentine 1.47. (a) In which is light fastest? (b) Which is optically densest? (c) Find the refractive index of turpentine with respect to water.

(a) Water (lowest n). 1 (b) Turpentine (highest n). 1 (c) 1.47/1.33 = 1.11. 1

C4One half of a convex lens is covered with black paper. Will it form a complete image of the object? Explain with a ray diagram.

Yes, a complete image is formed, but it is less bright. 1 Every point of the object sends rays to every part of the lens; the uncovered half still refracts rays from all points of the object to the image, only fewer of them. 1 (Diagram: the usual convex lens diagram with the lower half shaded; rays through the top half still meet at the image.) 1

Section D: 5 marks

D1(a) Define principal focus and focal length of a concave mirror. (b) An object 7 cm tall is placed 27 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 18 cm. At what distance should a screen be placed to get a sharp image? Find the size and nature of the image. (c) Draw the ray diagram.

(a) Point on the principal axis where rays parallel to it meet after reflection; distance PF. 1

(b) u = −27, f = −18. 1/v = −1/18 + 1/27 = (−3 + 2)/54 ⇒ v = −54 cm. 1½ m = −v/u = −2; h′ = −14 cm: real, inverted, enlarged. 1½

(c) Object between C and F ⇒ image beyond C. 1

D2(a) State the laws of refraction. (b) Draw the path of a ray through a rectangular glass slab and mark ∠i, ∠r, ∠e and the lateral displacement. (c) Why is the emergent ray parallel to the incident ray?

(a) Laws as in 9.3 (same plane; sin i/sin r = constant for a given pair of media and colour). 2 (b) See the glass slab diagram in section 9.3. 2 (c) The bending at the second face is equal and opposite to the bending at the first face because the faces are parallel, so ∠e = ∠i. 1

(i) Between the lens and its focus (closer than 12 cm). 1 (ii) Convex mirror: erect image and wide field of view. 1 (iii) u = −8, f = +12: 1/v = 1/12 − 1/8 = −1/24 ⇒ v = −24 cm; m = v/u = +3. Virtual, erect, 3 times magnified. 2

(i) Concave mirror. 1 (ii) At its principal focus. 1 (iii) f = −15 cm, u = −20 cm. 1/v = −1/15 + 1/20 = −1/60 ⇒ v = −60 cm: light converges to a point 60 cm in front (not parallel). 2

NCERT

All textbook questions answered

In-text questions (section 9.2)

Q1Define the principal focus of a concave mirror.
The point on the principal axis at which rays of light parallel to the principal axis meet after reflection from a concave mirror.
Q2The radius of curvature of a spherical mirror is 20 cm. What is its focal length?
f = R/2 = 10 cm.
Q3Name a mirror that can give an erect and enlarged image of an object.
Concave mirror (object between P and F).
Q4Why do we prefer a convex mirror as a rear-view mirror in vehicles?
It always gives an erect, diminished image and has a wider field of view because it curves outwards, so the driver can see a large area of traffic behind.

In-text questions (mirror formula)

Q1Find the focal length of a convex mirror whose radius of curvature is 32 cm.
f = R/2 = +16 cm.
Q2A concave mirror produces three times magnified (enlarged) real image of an object placed at 10 cm in front of it. Where is the image located?
m = −3 (real), u = −10 cm. −3 = −v/(−10) ⇒ v = −30 cm: 30 cm in front of the mirror.

In-text questions (refraction)

Q1A ray of light travelling in air enters obliquely into water. Does the light ray bend towards the normal or away from the normal? Why?
Towards the normal, because water is optically denser than air: light slows down in water.
Q2Light enters from air to glass having refractive index 1.50. What is the speed of light in the glass? (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s)
v = c/n = 3 × 10⁸/1.50 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s.
Q3Find out, from Table 9.3, the medium having highest optical density. Also find the medium with lowest optical density.
Highest: diamond (2.42). Lowest: air (1.0003).
Q4You are given kerosene, turpentine and water. In which of these does the light travel fastest?
Water, as it has the lowest refractive index (1.33) of the three.
Q5The refractive index of diamond is 2.42. What is the meaning of this statement?
The speed of light in diamond is 1/2.42 times its speed in vacuum (air), i.e. light travels 2.42 times slower in diamond.

In-text questions (lenses and power)

Q1Define 1 dioptre of power of a lens.
1 dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is 1 metre.
Q2A convex lens forms a real and inverted image of a needle at a distance of 50 cm from it. Where is the needle placed in front of the convex lens if the image is equal to the size of the object? Also find the power of the lens.
Same-size real image ⇒ object at 2F₁: u = −50 cm. Then 2f = 50 cm, f = 25 cm = 0.25 m, P = +4 D.
Q3Find the power of a concave lens of focal length 2 m.
P = 1/f = 1/(−2) = −0.5 D.
