Everything is made of atoms

A house and a human body look nothing alike. But zoom in far enough and both turn out to be built from the same tiny units: atoms. Atoms are so small that we can never see them with our eyes.

🤔 Big questions of this chapter

  • Is an atom really the smallest piece of matter, or can it be broken further?
  • Electrons are attracted by the protons in the nucleus. So why don’t they fall into it?
  • Why did scientists keep changing their picture (model) of the atom?
8.1

Where the idea of the atom began

More than 2,000 years ago, thinkers in India and in Greece asked the same question: what is everything made of?

Acharya KanadaAncient India

Keep dividing matter (dravya) and you reach particles that cannot be divided further: parmanu. His ideas are recorded in the Vaisheshika Sutras.

Leucippus and DemocritusAncient Greece

Called the uncuttable particles atomos (Greek for “indivisible”).

John Dalton1808

First scientific atomic theory: all matter is made of tiny, indivisible atoms, the building blocks of matter.

  • A parmanu is infinitely small and cannot be sensed. Their combinations make up the whole universe, including living bodies.
  • Gap in Kanada’s idea: it did not say in what proportions parmanus combine to form different substances.

💡 Key idea

The ideas of Kanada and Democritus came from thinking and imagination. Dalton’s theory was the first one based on experiments, so it became the starting point of modern atomic structure.

After Dalton, scientists wanted to know three things: what are atoms made of, what would an atom look like, and what makes the atoms of one element different from another?

8.2

A journey through atomic models

A model is a picture that scientists imagine to explain what they observe. Every new experiment tested the model. When it failed, the model was improved. The early models were not fully correct, but they show how science moves forward one step at a time.

Clue: atoms can give out particles

Until the late 1800s, atoms were thought to be indivisible. Then scientists found that some elements give out invisible energy and particles on their own. This is called radioactivity, and what comes out is radiation. If particles can come out of an atom, the atom must have smaller parts inside it.

Discovery of the electron (J. J. Thomson, 1897)

  • Thomson passed electricity through a gas at very low pressure in a glass tube with two electrodes, using a high voltage.
  • Rays travelled from the cathode (−) to the anode (+). These are cathode rays.
  • Electric and magnetic fields showed the rays are streams of negatively charged particles, much lighter than atoms. These particles were later named electrons.
  • The rays were the same whatever the cathode material or the gas in the tube. So electrons are present in the atoms of every element.
  • Charge on an electron = −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. For convenience it is taken as −1.

Note: atoms have no colour. Colours in these diagrams only help you tell the parts apart.

Thomson’s model: the plum pudding (watermelon) model

The puzzle: atoms are neutral, but electrons are negative. So where is the positive charge?

  • An atom is a sphere of positive charge with electrons spread through it, like plums in a pudding or seeds in a watermelon.
  • Total positive charge = total negative charge, so the atom is neutral.

💡 Why it matters

It was the first real attempt to show how the positive and negative charges in an atom balance each other. It was replaced when it failed to explain the gold foil experiment.

Testing Thomson’s model: the gold foil experiment (1911)

Geiger and Marsden, working under Ernest Rutherford, fired a narrow beam of alpha (α) particles at an extremely thin gold foil.

  • α-particles are tiny, positively charged particles given out by some radioactive elements. An α-particle is the nucleus of a helium atom: 2 protons + 2 neutrons.
  • Expected (if Thomson was right): the positive charge is spread out, so α-particles should go straight through or bend only a little.
What they sawWhat it means
① Most α-particles went straight throughMost of the atom is empty space
② Some were deflected, a few through large anglesThe positive charge is not spread out. It sits in a small region that pushes α-particles away
③ Very few bounced backThat region is tiny but very dense: it holds almost all the mass

Turning of a particle away from its straight path is called scattering, so this is also called the α-ray scattering experiment.

🧪 Think as a scientist: what if the foil were thicker?

A thicker foil has more layers of gold atoms, so each α-particle has more chances of meeting a nucleus. Fewer would pass straight, and more would be deflected or bounce back.

Rutherford’s nuclear (planetary) model

  • All the positive charge and almost all the mass is packed in a tiny centre called the nucleus.
  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus, like planets around the Sun. So it is called the planetary model.
  • Size of an atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m. Size of the nucleus ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m. The nucleus is about 10⁵ (one lakh) times smaller than the atom.

✨ Try this calculation

A page of your book is about 0.1 mm = 10⁻⁴ m thick. One atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m across. Atoms stacked across the page ≈ 10⁻⁴ ÷ 10⁻¹⁰ = 10⁶, that is about ten lakh atoms!

Where Rutherford’s model went wrong

  • Anything moving in a circle keeps changing direction, so it is accelerating (Chapter 4, Describing Motion Around Us).
  • An accelerating charged particle should keep losing energy. The electron would spiral inwards and fall into the nucleus, and the atom would collapse.
  • But atoms are stable; that is why matter around us stays intact. So the model could not explain the stability of the atom.

Discovery of the proton

  • Rutherford showed that the positive charge of the nucleus comes from particles he named protons.
  • A proton carries a charge equal and opposite to an electron (+1) and is much heavier than an electron.
  • In a neutral atom, number of protons = number of electrons. Helium: 2 and 2. Sodium: 11 and 11.

Bohr’s model (Niels Bohr, 1913)

  • Electrons move only in certain fixed circular paths called stationary states, orbits or shells.
  • Each shell has a definite energy, so shells are also called energy levels. They are named K, L, M, N… or numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4…
  • An electron can be in a shell, never between shells. While moving in its shell, it does not lose energy.
  • K (n = 1) is closest to the nucleus and has the lowest energy. Energy increases as you go outwards.
  • To jump to a higher shell an electron absorbs energy; to drop to a lower shell it releases energy. The amount is exactly the energy difference between the two shells.
  • Each shell can hold only a limited number of electrons.

💡 How Bohr explained stability

Bohr assumed (as a postulate) that in a stationary state the electron’s energy stays constant even though it is moving. No energy loss means no spiralling in, so the atom is stable.

🔤 Why K, L, M, N and not A, B, C?

Physicist Charles Barkla named the first X-ray line he observed “K”. He skipped A to J to leave room for any series found before K. None was ever found, and Bohr used the same letters for shells.

Bohr’s model also has limitations. It was later replaced by the quantum mechanical model, which you will study in higher classes.

8.3

What gives an atom its mass?

Electrons are so light that their mass can be ignored. So the mass of an atom must be in its nucleus. But there was a puzzle:

🤔 The helium puzzle

Hydrogen has 1 proton and helium has 2 protons. So helium should be about 2 times as heavy as hydrogen. Instead it is about 4 times as heavy. Something else in the nucleus must be adding mass without adding charge.

Discovery of the neutron (James Chadwick, 1932)

  • Chadwick, a student of Rutherford, found a particle with mass nearly equal to a proton but no charge. It was named the neutron (symbol n).
  • Neutrons are found in the nucleus of all atoms except ordinary hydrogen.
  • So the mass of an atom comes mainly from its protons and neutrons, packed tightly in the nucleus.

Why don’t the protons in a nucleus fly apart?

All protons are positive, so each one pushes every other one away. Neutrons help in two ways: they sit between the protons and increase the distance between them (less push), and they add to the nuclear force, a strong attraction that binds all the particles of the nucleus together.

✨ What if an atom had no empty space?

Everything would shrink enormously, because the nucleus is a lakh times smaller than the atom. If all the empty space were squeezed out of the atoms of every person on Earth, the whole of humanity would fit into roughly the size of a sugar cube!

⚛️ The atomic age

Having no charge, neutrons can enter nuclei easily. This led to man-made radioactive elements and to splitting uranium atoms, which gave us both nuclear power and nuclear weapons.

🇮🇳 India’s contribution

The Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), Mumbai, uses reactors like Dhruva for neutron-scattering experiments. This research helps develop superconductors, battery electrodes, medicines and industrial alloys in India. Find out more about Dhruva!

By 1869 scientists knew about 69 elements. Today we know 118 elements, some of them made artificially.

8.4

Symbols of elements

  • Dalton (1803) was the first to use symbols: he drew pictures for elements.
  • Berzelius (1813) suggested using letters taken from the Latin names of elements.
  • Today, IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) approves the names and symbols of elements.

Rules for writing symbols

  1. Many symbols are the first letter or first two letters of the name: H, C, O, Al, Ca, Ne.
  2. The first letter is always capital and the second letter is small: Al (not AL), Co (not CO).
  3. Some use the first letter and a later letter of the name: Cl (chlorine), Zn (zinc), Mg (magnesium).
  4. Some come from Latin, Greek or German names: Fe from ferrum, Hg from hydrargyros (Greek), W (tungsten) from wolfram (German).

⚠️ Exam alert

Co is cobalt, an element. CO is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen. One capital letter changes everything!

8.5

Atomic number and mass number

Atomic number (Z)

The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It is written as Z. It decides which element the atom is and how it behaves chemically.

  • An atom is neutral, so protons = electrons. Hydrogen: Z = 1 (1 proton, 1 electron). Helium: Z = 2 (2 protons, 2 electrons).
  • Every element has its own atomic number. Change the number of protons and you get a different element.

Mass number (A)

The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. It is written as A. Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons. Electrons are so light that they are not counted.

ElementProtons (p⁺)Neutrons (n⁰)Mass number (A)
Hydrogen101
Helium224
Lithium347

This solves the helium puzzle: 2 protons + 2 neutrons give helium a mass number of 4.

How to write an atom

Z = number of protons = number of electronsin a neutral atom
A = protons + neutrons
Neutrons = A − Z

✏️ Solved example

For 2311Na: protons = 11, electrons = 11, neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12.
For 3517Cl: protons = 17, electrons = 17, neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18.

⚠️ Exam alert

Mass number is a count of particles, so it is always a whole number. Do not add electrons to it.

8.7

How electrons are arranged in shells

Bohr and Bury gave three simple rules for filling electrons into shells:

  1. The maximum number of electrons a shell can hold is 2n², where n is the shell number.
  2. The outermost shell can hold at most 8 electrons (the K shell alone holds at most 2).
  3. Shells fill step by step from the inside out: K first, then L, then M, and so on. The L shell starts filling only after K is full.

The way electrons are distributed among the shells is called the electronic configuration. Hydrogen’s single electron sits in K. Helium’s two electrons both fit in K, which is now full. Each next element adds one more electron to the right shell.

🤔 Think: why is potassium 2, 8, 8, 1?

Potassium has 19 electrons. The M shell can hold 18, so why not 2, 8, 9? Rule 2: the outermost shell cannot hold more than 8. So the 19th electron starts a new shell, N.

8.8

Combining capacity of an atom: valency

The combining capacity of an element is the number of hydrogen or chlorine atoms that one atom of it combines with. Hydrogen and chlorine are used as the measuring stick because each has a combining capacity of 1.

CompoundOne atom of…joins withCombining capacity
H₂O (water)oxygen2 H2
NH₃ (ammonia)nitrogen3 H3
MgCl₂ (magnesium chloride)magnesium2 Cl2
CH₄ (methane)carbon4 H4

Where valency comes from

  • The outermost shell that has electrons is the valence shell. Its electrons are the valence electrons.
  • 8 electrons in the outermost shell is called an octet. Atoms with a complete octet (or 2 electrons, in helium’s case) are stable and largely unreactive.
  • Other atoms lose, gain or share electrons to complete their octet.
  • Valency = the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares to complete its octet.

Some compounds do not follow this simple rule (phosphorus, for example, can show valency 3 or 5). You will learn why in higher classes.

8.9

A deeper look: isotopes and isobars

Isotopes

Dalton believed all atoms of an element are identical and have the same mass. Scientists later found atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons.

💡 Definition

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Think of them as “twin atoms”.

PropertyIsotopes of an elementWhy
Chemical propertiesSameSame number of electrons and same configuration; chemistry depends on valence electrons
Physical propertiesDifferent (mass, melting and boiling points)Different number of neutrons, so different mass

Isotopes at work

IsotopeUse
23592U (uranium)Fuel in nuclear reactors to produce electricity
6027Co (cobalt)Radiation treatment of cancer
13153I (iodine)Treatment of goitre and thyroid cancer
146C (carbon)Finding the age of fossils and ancient objects

⚖️ Unit of atomic mass

Atoms are far too light to weigh in grams, just as you would not weigh a grain of wheat in kilograms. Atomic masses are measured in the unified atomic mass unit (u). It was earlier written as amu.

Average atomic mass

Chlorine has two isotopes, ³⁵Cl (mass 35 u) and ³⁷Cl (mass 37 u), found in nature in the ratio 3 : 1, that is 75% and 25%. So what is the mass of “a chlorine atom”?

Simple average = (35 + 37) ÷ 2 = 36 uWrong for nature: it treats both isotopes as equally common
Weighted average = 35 × 75/100 + 37 × 25/100 = 105/4 + 37/4 = 142/4 = 35.5 uCorrect: each mass is multiplied by its percentage abundance

No single chlorine atom weighs 35.5 u. In 10 lakh chlorine atoms, about 7.5 lakh are ³⁵Cl and 2.5 lakh are ³⁷Cl, and their average comes to 35.5 u.

Isobars

Calcium (Z = 20), potassium (Z = 19) and argon (Z = 18) are different elements, yet each can have a mass number of 40: the total number of nucleons is the same.

💡 Definition

Isobars are atoms of different elements with the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

The journey is not over

Even Bohr’s model is not fully correct. Electrons do not move in neat fixed orbits; they exist as electron clouds around the nucleus. We can predict where an electron is most likely to be, not exactly where it is. Today, electron microscopes such as the STM and TEM can even show individual atoms.

Quick revision

  • Atoms are the building blocks of all matter, living and non-living.
  • Thomson: electrons are embedded in a sphere of positive charge.
  • Rutherford: the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus at the centre; electrons revolve around it. It could not explain stability.
  • Bohr: electrons move in fixed shells (K, L, M, N…) without losing energy.
  • Three subatomic particles: electron (−1), proton (+1), neutron (0, found by Chadwick).
  • Atomic number Z = protons. Mass number A = protons + neutrons (nucleons).
  • Shell capacity is 2n²; the outermost shell holds at most 8; fill K → L → M.
  • An outermost shell with 8 electrons (2 for helium) is stable and largely unreactive.
  • Valency = electrons lost, gained or shared to reach a stable octet.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different A. Isobars: same A, different Z.
  • Average atomic mass takes into account how common each isotope is in nature.

⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing CO for cobalt. The symbol is Co.
  • Adding electrons to the mass number.
  • Confusing mass number (always a whole number) with average atomic mass (can be a decimal, like 35.5 u).
  • Writing potassium as 2, 8, 9. The outermost shell cannot have more than 8, so it is 2, 8, 8, 1.
  • Saying every nucleus has neutrons. Ordinary hydrogen has none.
  • Saying the gold foil experiment found neutrons. It found the nucleus; neutrons came in 1932.
  • Saying isotopes have different chemical properties. They are the same.

Pause and Ponder (in-text questions)

1Thomson’s atom made of clay (positive) and beads (electrons): what if (i) the clay’s positive charge is less than the beads’ total negative charge, (ii) the clay itself carries some negative charge?
(i) The negative charge would be more than the positive charge, so the model would have an overall negative charge. It would not be a neutral atom.
(ii) No. The clay’s negative charge adds to the beads’ negative charge, so total negative charge becomes more than total positive charge. A neutral atom needs the two to be exactly equal.
2Is an orange or a lemon (seeds inside soft pulp) a good comparison for Thomson’s model?
Where it matches: the seeds (electrons) sit inside a soft body (positive charge) that fills the whole fruit.
Where it falls short: the seeds are bunched in the centre and inside segments instead of being spread evenly; the fruit has a peel and segments that the model does not have; and nothing in a fruit carries charge, so it cannot show how charges balance.
3Why did Thomson conclude that electrons are present in all atoms?
Cathode rays behaved the same whatever material the cathode was made of and whatever gas filled the tube. The same negative particles were coming out of every kind of atom, so electrons must be a part of all atoms.
4What would happen if α-particles were replaced by negatively charged particles in the gold foil experiment?
The nucleus is positive, so negative particles would be attracted towards it instead of being pushed away. Most would still pass straight through the empty space. Those passing close to a nucleus would be pulled towards it and swing around it, so their paths would bend towards the nucleus.
5How does the bouncing back of a few α-particles rule out the plum pudding model?
In Thomson’s model the positive charge and mass are spread thinly over the whole atom. Such a spread-out charge can only nudge a fast, heavy α-particle a little; it can never stop it and throw it back. Bouncing back is possible only if the α-particle meets a tiny region with a lot of positive charge and mass packed together, that is, a nucleus. Thomson’s model has no such region, so it must be wrong.
6If you could ask Rutherford one question, what would it be?
Your own question is the answer here. Some ideas: “Why did you choose gold?” (Gold can be hammered into extremely thin sheets.) “How did you see where the α-particles went?” (Each one made a tiny flash of light on a zinc sulphide screen, and the flashes were counted.) “What did you think when you first saw particles bouncing back?”
7Assertion: Rutherford concluded that most of the mass of an atom is in a small central region, the nucleus. Reason: In Thomson’s model, electrons are embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge.
Option (ii). Both statements are true, but the Reason describes Thomson’s model, so it does not explain Rutherford’s conclusion.
8Name a new element after yourself and show that its symbol follows IUPAC rules.
Example: a student named Aarav could call it Aaravium, symbol Av. It follows the rules: the first letter is capital and the second is small, the letters come from the name, and no other element already uses Av. (A symbol such as Ar would not work: argon already has it.)
9What problems could arise if every scientist used different symbols for the same element?
The same symbol could mean different elements to different people, so formulas and equations could not be shared or checked across countries. This would cause confusion and dangerous mistakes in labs, medicine and industry. A single IUPAC system avoids this.
10An atom has atomic number 26 and 56 nucleons. Find its electrons, protons and neutrons.
Protons = 26, electrons = 26, neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30. (This is iron.)
11A nucleus has 20 protons and mass number 41. How many neutrons?
Neutrons = 41 − 20 = 21.
12An atom has 18 neutrons and atomic number 17. Find its mass number.
Mass number = 17 + 18 = 35.
13An atom with mass number 23 has 11 electrons. Find its neutrons.
Electrons = protons = 11, so neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12.
14Electrons in the outermost shell of carbon-12, fluorine-19 and silicon-28?
Carbon (Z = 6): 2, 4, so 4. Fluorine (Z = 9): 2, 7, so 7. Silicon (Z = 14): 2, 8, 4, so 4.
15Write the electronic configuration of elements with atomic numbers 12, 16 and 18.
Z = 12 (magnesium): 2, 8, 2. Z = 16 (sulfur): 2, 8, 6. Z = 18 (argon): 2, 8, 8.
16Riddle: mass number 23, 11 protons, a soft metal that reacts vigorously with water. Who am I, and how many neutrons?
Sodium (Na), with 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.
A riddle of your own: “I have 12 protons and 12 neutrons. I burn with a dazzling white light and I sit at the centre of chlorophyll. Who am I?” (Magnesium)
17Two atoms each have 11 protons; one has 12 neutrons, the other 13. Compare them.
Atomic number: both 11 (same). Mass numbers: 23 and 24 (different). They are the same element (sodium), so they are isotopes.
18Bromine has two isotopes: bromine-79 (49.7%) and bromine-81 (50.3%). Find its average atomic mass.
Average = 79 × 49.7/100 + 81 × 50.3/100 = 39.263 + 40.743 = 80.006 u ≈ 80 u.

★How many electrons do protium, deuterium and tritium have?
One each, because each has one proton.
★Will atoms that already have 8 outer electrons lose or gain electrons?
No. Their octet is already complete, so they are stable and unreactive. Their valency is 0 (helium, neon, argon).

Revise, Reflect, Refine (exercises)

1Gold foil experiment: which statements are correct? (i) It showed neutrons in the nucleus. (ii) It disproved the plum pudding model and led to a central nucleus. (iii) Large deflections showed that most mass and positive charge are in a tiny centre. (iv) The deflections showed that electrons move around the nucleus.
Correct: (ii) and (iii).
(i) Incorrect: neutrons have no charge and were found later, by Chadwick in 1932.
(ii) Correct: a spread-out positive charge cannot turn α-particles through large angles, so a central nucleus was needed.
(iii) Correct: only a tiny, heavy, concentrated positive centre can deflect fast α-particles so strongly.
(iv) Incorrect: the deflections were caused by the nucleus. Electrons are far too light to turn α-particles, so the experiment told nothing about how electrons move.
2Bohr’s model: correct or incorrect? (i) Electrons lose energy in fixed orbits and fall into the nucleus. (ii) Electrons can be anywhere with no fixed energy. (iii) Electrons revolve in orbits of fixed energy without losing energy. (iv) Electrons can be found between energy levels.
(i) Incorrect: in a fixed orbit an electron does not lose energy, so it never falls in.
(ii) Incorrect: electrons can be only in certain shells, each with a fixed energy.
(iii) Correct: this is Bohr’s main idea.
(iv) Incorrect: an electron is never between levels; it jumps from one level to another by absorbing or releasing energy.
3Nuclei: X (18 p, 19 n), Y (17 p, 18 n), Z (17 p, 20 n). Relation between (i) Y and Z, (ii) Z and X?
X: Z = 18, A = 37. Y: Z = 17, A = 35. Z: Z = 17, A = 37.
(i) Y and Z: same atomic number (17), different mass numbers (35 and 37), so they are isotopes (of chlorine).
(ii) Z and X: same mass number (37), different atomic numbers (17 and 18), so they are isobars.
4What did Rutherford conclude about the positive part of the atom from the α-particles that bounced back or turned through large angles?
The positive part is at the centre of the atom, takes up a very tiny volume, and holds all the positive charge and almost all the mass, so it is extremely dense. He called it the nucleus.
5Arrange the models in the order they were proposed and explain.
(iv) → (ii) → (iii) → (i)
Dalton (atoms indivisible) → Thomson (after the electron was found: electrons in a positive sphere) → Rutherford (gold foil experiment: dense central nucleus) → Bohr (fixed orbits to explain why atoms are stable). Each model fixed a problem in the one before it.
6Why don’t electrons fly away from the atom?
Electrons are negative and the nucleus is positive, so the nucleus attracts them. This pull towards the centre keeps them moving around the nucleus, just as the Sun’s gravity keeps the planets in orbit. In Bohr’s model they stay in their fixed shells without losing energy, so they neither fly away nor fall in.
7Assertion: The discovery of subatomic particles helped in understanding atomic structure. Reason: An atom has equal numbers of electrons and protons.
Option (ii). Both are true, but equal numbers of electrons and protons is just one fact about atoms; it does not explain how the discoveries helped.
8Magnesium atom with mass number 24 and atomic number 12: find protons, neutrons, electrons and draw its electron arrangement.
Protons = 12, neutrons = 24 − 12 = 12, electrons = 12. Arrangement: 2, 8, 2 (K 2, L 8, M 2).

10Both models have orbiting electrons. Why did Rutherford’s fail to explain stability while Bohr’s succeeded?
In Rutherford’s model nothing stops the revolving electron from losing energy. Because it is always accelerating, it should lose energy, spiral in and crash into the nucleus. Bohr postulated fixed stationary orbits in which the electron’s energy stays constant; its energy changes only when it jumps between shells. With no energy loss, the atom stays stable.
11An atom with mass number 70 has 31 electrons. How many neutrons?
Protons = 31, so neutrons = 70 − 31 = 39.
12An atom has 79 protons and mass number 197. Find (i) neutrons, (ii) electrons.
(i) Neutrons = 197 − 79 = 118. (ii) Electrons = 79. (This is gold.)

15An atom has 12 protons and 12 neutrons. If all its electrons were replaced by particles with the same charge but 500 times heavier, what happens to its (i) atomic number, (ii) atomic mass, (iii) mass number, (iv) overall charge?
(i) No change: still 12 protons, so Z = 12.
(ii) Increases. An electron’s mass is about 1/1836 u; 500 times that is about 0.27 u. Twelve such particles add about 3.3 u, so the mass rises from about 24 u to about 27.3 u.
(iii) No change: mass number counts only protons and neutrons, so it stays 24.
(iv) No change: 12 positive and 12 equal negative charges, so the atom stays neutral.
